How to Make Video Calls With Grandparents Actually Work

A practical guide to setting up video calls grandparents can actually use—devices, apps, lighting, and the mistakes that cause most calls to fail.
October 14, 2026 · 7 min · 1451 words · TechLife Guide

Introduction#

My mother-in-law answered her first FaceTime call by holding the iPad two inches from her ear, screen facing out, and shouting “I can hear you but I can’t see you!” for eleven minutes. The problem wasn’t her. It was us. We’d handed her a device, tapped an icon, and assumed the rest would follow. It didn’t.

This happens in millions of households, and it’s almost always fixable with about 90 minutes of setup and a few decisions made before the first call. What follows is the version of this guide I wish someone had given me four years ago—specific devices, specific settings, specific scripts, and the failure points that cause most grandparent video calls to quietly stop happening after week three.

Start With the Right Device, Not the Right App#

Most families argue about Zoom vs. FaceTime vs. WhatsApp. That’s the wrong argument. The device matters more than the app, because the device determines how many steps your grandparent has to remember.

The hierarchy of ease, from easiest to hardest:

  1. A dedicated tablet on a stand (iPad 9th gen, ~$250 refurbished, or Amazon Fire HD 10, ~$140). Screen stays on, app stays open, one tap to answer.
  2. A smart display (Amazon Echo Show 8, ~$150; Meta Portal Go, discontinued but common secondhand). These win on one specific feature: voice answering. “Alexa, answer the call” requires zero tapping.
  3. A laptop with a permanently open browser tab. Workable, but trackpad fumbling kills momentum.
  4. A smartphone. Worst option for anyone over 80 with arthritis or vision issues. Small screen, easy to accidentally hang up, notifications interrupt.

If your grandparent already uses a smartphone comfortably, ignore this ranking—familiarity beats ergonomics. But if they’re calling you from a flip phone, buy them a tablet. A refurbished iPad with a $35 Logitech stand and a $20 stylus is a better investment than any app subscription.

The 20-Minute Setup Checklist#

Do this in person if possible. If not, do it over a regular phone call while they sit in front of the device. Budget 20 minutes; it usually takes 40 the first time.

Before you arrive or call:

  • Create the account yourself (Apple ID, Google, or Amazon). Use a password you manage. Write it in a paper notebook they keep near the device.
  • Disable two-factor authentication prompts on the device itself, or set them to route to your phone.
  • Turn off auto-lock or set it to 30 minutes. Settings > Display & Brightness > Auto-Lock.
  • Turn off notifications for everything except the calling app. A weather alert mid-call confuses people.
  • Set text size to large. Settings > Accessibility > Display & Text Size > Larger Text.
  • Increase contrast and enable “Bold Text.”
  • Set the volume to 80% and lock it if the device allows (Guided Access on iPad does this).

During setup:

  • Install exactly one calling app. Not two. One.
  • Add yourself and 3–4 other family members as favorites, with photos.
  • Make a test call from another room while they watch.
  • Have them answer the call themselves, twice.
  • Have them hang up themselves, twice. This is the step everyone skips.
  • Write the steps on an index card in 18-point font: “1. Tap my photo. 2. Talk. 3. Tap red button to end.”

That index card sounds patronizing. It isn’t. My father-in-law used his for eight months and then stopped needing it.

Lighting, Sound, and Framing: The Stuff Nobody Mentions#

A $900 iPad Pro in a dim hallway produces a worse call than a $140 Fire tablet next to a window at 3 p.m.

Lighting: Put the light source in front of your grandparent, not behind them. A window works. A $25 ring light on a floor lamp works better. Backlighting turns them into a silhouette, which is the single most common reason older adults say “I look terrible on these things.”

Sound: Tablet microphones pick up room echo badly. A $30 wired earbud with an inline mic (any brand—Anker, Panasonic) solves 80% of “I can’t hear you” complaints. If they refuse earbuds, put a rug or towel on the hard floor near where they sit. Soft surfaces absorb echo.

Framing: Prop the tablet at eye level using a stack of books or a $15 gooseneck stand. Camera below chin level is unflattering for everyone and worse for anyone with a double chin they’re self-conscious about. Distance: 2–3 feet from face. Closer feels invasive; farther makes them a tiny figure in a big room.

Internet: If calls freeze, the problem is usually upload speed, not download. Run a speed test at their house. Anything under 3 Mbps upload will stutter. A $60 mesh extender (TP-Link RE220) fixes most dead-zone issues. If they’re on DSL, no extender will help—consider a hotspot or a cheap 5G home internet plan if available.

What Most Guides Get Wrong#

1. They optimize for the caller, not the answerer. Every guide tells you how to start a call. Almost none address the fact that your grandparent has to accept it, often while doing something else, often with reading glasses across the room. Solve for the receiving end: auto-answer features (FaceTime has it for approved contacts; Echo Show has Drop In), big buttons, and a device that lives in one predictable spot.

2. They assume frequency is the goal. Weekly 45-minute calls fail. Daily 4-minute calls succeed. Short, boring, frequent calls build the habit and the muscle memory. My grandmother and I do a 3-minute “good morning” call most days. She’s better at the technology now than she was after six months of Sunday marathons.

3. They ignore the “who calls whom” problem. If your grandparent never initiates, they never learn. Rotate: they call you on Tuesdays, you call them on Fridays. Yes, it’s slower. Yes, it’s worth it.

The Myth: “They’re Too Old to Learn This”#

This is the most common thing I hear, and it’s wrong in a specific way. What older adults struggle with isn’t learning—it’s relearning. A 78-year-old who has used a landline for 50 years has a deeply grooved mental model of how a phone works. Video calling breaks three of its rules: you don’t dial, you don’t hold it to your ear, and you can see the other person. That’s genuinely disorienting.

The fix isn’t repetition. It’s anchoring new behavior to old behavior. “It’s like a phone, but you put it on the table and look at it” works better than any tutorial. So does leaving the device in the same spot as the old landline. My mother-in-law’s iPad lives where her corded phone sat for 30 years. She found it instantly.

Also: stop saying “just tap.” “Just” is a word that makes people feel stupid. Say “tap the picture of my face.”

A Realistic Call Script for the First Month#

Week 1: Two 5-minute calls, both initiated by you, at the same time each day. Goal: they answer and hang up unaided. Week 2: Three calls. One initiated by them (you prompt via text or a phone call: “Call me on the tablet in 10 minutes”). Week 3: Add a second family member. Same device, different face. Goal: they can choose between two contacts. Week 4: One call where they show you something—a plant, a photo, the dog. This transforms the tablet from a phone into a window. That shift is what makes the habit stick.

Expect one total failure week. It happens. Don’t troubleshoot mid-call; hang up, call on the regular phone, fix it later.

When It Still Doesn’t Work#

Some situations genuinely defeat video calling: significant hearing loss without hearing aids, moderate-to-severe dementia, or no reliable internet. In those cases, stop forcing it.

Better alternatives:

  • Voice-only calls on the same tablet, which removes the “where do I look” problem.
  • A shared photo frame (Aura Carver, ~$150) that family members send photos to via app. No calls, no answering, but daily connection.
  • Recorded video messages sent via text or email. Your grandparent watches on their own schedule. Some people prefer this permanently, and that’s fine.

The goal was never video calls. The goal was your grandmother seeing your kid lose a tooth. Use whatever tool does that.

The One Rule That Matters#

Call at the same time, on the same day, using the same device, in the same spot. Predictability beats features. A $140 Fire tablet on a Tuesday schedule will outlast a $1,200 iPad Pro used sporadically, every single time.

Start this week. Not with a perfect setup—with a five-minute call where the only goal is that they answer and you both say hello. Everything else is refinement.
