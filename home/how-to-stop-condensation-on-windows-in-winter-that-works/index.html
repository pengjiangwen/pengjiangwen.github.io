How to Stop Condensation on Windows in Winter (That Works)

Stop window condensation this winter with proven fixes: ventilation rates, humidity targets, double-glazing upgrades, and the myths that make it worse.
November 5, 2026 · 8 min · 1601 words · TechLife Guide

Introduction#

At 7:10 on a January morning, you pull back the bedroom curtain and find the glass running with water. By the time you’ve made coffee, that water has dripped onto the sill, soaked into the paint, and started the slow process that ends with black mould in the corner within three weeks.

Here’s the number that matters: a family of four produces roughly 10–15 litres of moisture per day just by breathing, cooking, showering, and drying laundry indoors. In winter, that moisture has nowhere to go. It finds the coldest surface in the house — usually a single-glazed window or a north-facing wall — and condenses there.

Most advice on this problem is vague (“improve ventilation,” “reduce humidity”). This guide gives you the actual numbers, products, and sequence of fixes, ordered from cheapest to most expensive, plus the mistakes that make condensation worse.

First, understand what you’re actually fighting#

Condensation forms when warm, moist air hits a surface below its dew point. At 20°C indoor air with 60% relative humidity, the dew point is about 12°C. Any window pane colder than that will fog, then run.

Two levers exist, and only two:

  1. Reduce the moisture in the air (lower humidity)
  2. Raise the surface temperature of the glass (better glazing, better air circulation)

Everything below pulls one of those two levers. Anything that does neither — and plenty of popular advice falls into this category — is wasted money.

Step 1: Measure before you fix anything#

Don’t guess at your humidity. Buy a hygrometer — a basic digital one costs £8–£15 (a ThermoPro TP49 or similar). Put it in the coldest room, not next to the radiator.

Target indoor relative humidity in winter: 40–50%. Below 40% and you’ll get dry throats and shrinking woodwork; above 60% and you’re feeding mould and window condensation. If your bedroom reads 65%+ on a cold morning, that’s your problem, not the windows.

Also check: does the condensation appear only on one window, or all of them? One window usually means that pane is the coldest surface (poor seal, single glazing, or a blocked radiator underneath). All windows means whole-house humidity is too high.

Step 2: The free fixes (do these first, in this order)#

Open windows properly, not constantly#

The old advice to “crack a window all day” wastes heat and doesn’t work well. Do this instead: shock ventilation. Open windows wide for 5–10 minutes, 2–3 times a day, especially after cooking and showering. This exchanges the air without cooling the walls and furniture, which is what happens with a window left ajar for hours.

Best times: first thing in the morning (you’ve been breathing in a closed room all night) and right after any steam-producing activity.

Run extractor fans for longer than you think#

A typical bathroom extractor runs at 15–25 litres per second. For a 6m² bathroom, that clears the steam from a shower in about 10–15 minutes — but most people switch it off after two. Leave it running 15 minutes after a shower, and leave the door closed so you’re not pulling moist air into the rest of the house.

Check your kitchen extractor too. If it vents into the room (recirculating type) rather than outside, it does nothing for humidity. A ducted hood venting outdoors is the only kind that removes moisture.

Stop drying laundry indoors — or ventilate for it#

A single load of washing holds roughly 2–3 litres of water. Drying it on a radiator in a closed room dumps all of that into your air. If you must dry indoors, use a condensing tumble dryer (a heat-pump model uses about 0.5–1 kWh per cycle) or dry in a room with the window open and the door shut.

Move furniture and clear radiators#

A sofa pushed against a radiator blocks the convective loop that warms the window above it. Keep at least 10cm clearance under windowsills and in front of radiators. This alone fixes a surprising number of single-window condensation cases.

Step 3: The cheap hardware fixes (£10–£60)#

Window vacs actually work#

A Kärcher WV 1 (about £50) or a generic equivalent sucks water off glass in seconds. It doesn’t solve the cause, but it stops water running onto sills and frames, which is what causes paint failure and mould. Empty the tank after each use — standing water in the unit smells within days.

Dehumidifiers: buy the right size#

A small Peltier (thermoelectric) dehumidifier at £30–£50 removes maybe 300ml per day. That’s not enough for a family home. A proper compressor dehumidifier — 10–12 litres per day extraction, £120–£180 — will hold a 3-bed house at 50% humidity through winter for roughly £1–£2 per week in electricity. Run it in the coldest, dampest room with doors open, or in the room where laundry dries.

Desiccant dehumidifiers cost more to run but work better in unheated rooms like garages and conservatories, where compressor units lose efficiency below about 15°C.

Keep the heating steady#

Counterintuitive but true: intermittent heating makes condensation worse. A house that drops to 12°C overnight and is blasted to 21°C at 7am has cold walls and warm, moist air — perfect condensation conditions. A steady 18–19°C, even overnight, keeps surfaces above the dew point. If you’re worried about cost, 18°C around the clock is often cheaper than big reheating cycles anyway.

Step 4: The structural fixes (£100–£8,000)#

Fix the draughts, but don’t seal everything#

Draught-proofing a window with a £5 roll of self-adhesive foam strip stops cold air washing the glass — but if you seal a room completely (new windows, sealed vents, blocked chimney), you trap moisture. Every sealed room needs either a trickle vent, an extractor, or a daily shock-ventilation habit. Modern double glazing has trickle vents built in for exactly this reason; keep them open.

Secondary glazing: the best value upgrade#

If you have single glazing and can’t replace it (renting, listed building, budget), DIY secondary glazing film (window insulation kits, £10–£15 per window) raises the inner pane temperature by several degrees and stops most condensation. Rigid acrylic secondary glazing panels, professionally fitted, cost £150–£400 per window and perform better. Either beats doing nothing.

Double and triple glazing#

A modern double-glazed unit with a warm-edge spacer and argon fill keeps the inner pane well above the dew point in normal conditions. Expect £300–£700 per window fitted. Note: new, well-sealed windows often make condensation worse in the first winter if you don’t ventilate, because you’ve removed the accidental draughts that were drying the house. Budget for trickle vents or a positive input ventilation (PIV) unit at the same time.

PIV units: the whole-house fix#

A positive input ventilation unit (brands like Nuaire Drimaster, around £400–£800 installed) pushes filtered, slightly warmed air from the loft into the house, gently pressurising it and pushing moist air out through existing gaps. It runs at about 10–30W — pennies a day. For houses with persistent whole-house condensation and no mechanical ventilation, this is often the single most effective fix.

Checklist: your winter condensation routine#

Daily

  • Shock-ventilate 5–10 minutes morning and evening (windows wide open)
  • Run bathroom extractor 15 minutes after every shower
  • Run kitchen extractor during and 10 minutes after cooking
  • Wipe down any running windows with a window vac or cloth
  • Keep internal doors open where possible so air circulates

Weekly

  • Check hygrometer readings in the coldest room — target 40–50%
  • Empty and clean the dehumidifier tank
  • Check windowsills and frames for early mould (black speckling)

Seasonally (before winter)

  • Test extractor fans with a tissue — it should hold against the grille
  • Clear radiator tops and windowsills of clutter
  • Check trickle vents are open, not painted shut
  • Re-seal any failed window seals or cracked putty

What most guides get wrong#

1. “Just open a window for ten minutes a day.” Not enough, and not specific enough. A family of four needs the air changed several times daily in wet rooms and at least twice house-wide. Vague ventilation advice is why people follow it and still get mould.

2. “Condensation means your windows are bad.” Often the opposite. New, airtight windows reduce draughts and can increase condensation because moisture no longer escapes. The windows are the messenger, not the cause. Replacing them without addressing ventilation is a £5,000 mistake.

3. “Put a bowl of salt / cat litter / charcoal near the window.” These absorb a few grams of water at best. A single shower puts 500g of water vapour into the air. Passive absorbents are a rounding error. Use a real dehumidifier or fix the ventilation.

4. The radiator myth. Some guides say “don’t put curtains over radiators because the warm air causes condensation.” Warm air doesn’t cause condensation — cold surfaces do. What curtains over radiators actually do is block heat from reaching the glass, making the window colder. Same outcome, wrong explanation, and the fix is to shorten curtains to sill level, not to turn the radiator off.

The one-week plan#

If you do nothing else: buy a hygrometer today (£10), note your bedroom humidity tomorrow morning, and if it’s over 60%, start shock-ventilating twice daily and running the bathroom fan for 15 minutes. If it’s still over 60% after a week, buy a 10-litre compressor dehumidifier and run it in the coldest room. That combination — measurement, ventilation habit, and mechanical dehumidification — solves the majority of winter window condensation without touching a single pane of glass.

The glass is rarely the problem. The air is.
