How to Stop Drafts From Your Front Door (For Good)

Stop front door drafts with weatherstripping, door sweeps, and threshold fixes. Real costs, measurements, and the mistakes that waste your money.
November 26, 2026 · 7 min · 1481 words · TechLife Guide

Introduction#

Stick your hand two inches from the bottom corner of your front door on a windy January night. If you feel moving air, you’re losing more than comfort—you’re paying to heat the neighborhood. A continuous 1/8-inch gap under a standard 36-inch door adds up to roughly 20 square inches of open hole, about the area of a large index card, letting cold air pour in every second the wind blows. Most people reach for a draft snake and call it done. That’s the equivalent of putting a bandage on a leaky pipe. This guide covers what actually seals a door: how to diagnose where the air is really coming from, which products work and what they cost, and the specific mistakes that make most DIY draft fixes fail within a season.

First, Find the Actual Leak#

Air almost never enters where you think it does. Before buying anything, run this 20-minute diagnostic.

The incense test#

Light a stick of incense and slowly trace it around the door’s perimeter—along the top, both sides, the bottom, and around the frame and hinges. Watch the smoke. It will bend toward any gap with airflow. Do this on a windy day if possible, or run a bathroom exhaust fan to depressurize the house slightly, which exaggerates leaks.

The dollar bill test#

Close the door on a dollar bill at several points around the frame. If you can pull the bill out without resistance, the weatherstripping isn’t contacting the door. Do this at the latch side, hinge side, top, and both bottom corners. Note which spots are loose—this tells you whether you need new weatherstripping, an adjustable threshold, or both.

The flashlight test (from outside)#

At night, have someone inside with the lights off while you run a bright flashlight along the exterior seams. Light bleeding through tells you exactly where gaps exist.

Write down what you find. Most front doors have two or three distinct leak points, not one, and each needs a different fix.

The Four Leak Zones (and What Each Actually Needs)#

1. The bottom gap: door sweep or threshold#

The bottom of the door is usually the biggest offender. You have two options:

A door sweep attaches to the door’s bottom face and drags or brushes against the threshold. Basic brush sweeps run $8–$15 at any hardware store. A rigid vinyl or rubber sweep with an aluminum carrier ($15–$30) seals better and lasts longer. Install time: 15 minutes with a hacksaw and a drill.

An adjustable threshold replaces the sill piece itself and has set screws that raise or lower a rubber seal to meet the door. Expect $25–$60 for a quality aluminum threshold (M-D Building Products and Frost King both make reliable ones). This is the better fix if your existing threshold is worn, cracked, or has a gap you can see daylight through. Install time: 45–60 minutes, and you’ll need to trim it to width with a hacksaw.

The mistake to avoid: Installing a sweep without checking that the door clears the floor across its full swing. Doors sag. A sweep set to the lowest point will scrape everywhere else, tear within weeks, and leave you sealing nothing. Close the door and check clearance at the latch side, center, and hinge side before you cut anything.

2. The sides and top: weatherstripping#

This is where product choice matters more than most guides admit.

  • V-strip (tension seal), $8–$12 per door: A folded strip that compresses between door and frame. Best for doors with even gaps. Fails on uneven gaps because it can’t expand far.
  • Foam tape, $5–$10: Cheap and easy, but compresses permanently within a season or two. Fine for a rental or a quick fix, not a permanent solution.
  • Magnetic weatherstripping, $20–$40: Works like a refrigerator seal. Excellent performance, but only if your door and frame are flat and true. On an old, warped door it will gap.
  • Door jamb seal kits with a kerf slot, $15–$35: These slide into a groove routed into the jamb. The most durable option, but requires the door to have that groove—many newer doors do.

Clean the surface with rubbing alcohol before applying any adhesive-backed strip. Skip this and you’ll be re-gluing it by spring. Apply in temperatures above 50°F; adhesive won’t bond properly in the cold.

3. The frame and rough opening: caulk and foam#

If the draft persists with the door sealed, air is coming through the wall around the frame—not the door itself. Check the interior trim for gaps and feel for air at the corners. A bead of paintable latex caulk ($5–$8 a tube) along interior trim gaps takes ten minutes. On the exterior, check the caulk line where the frame meets siding; failed caulk there is a common and overlooked leak.

For larger voids behind the trim, low-expansion spray foam (a window-and-door formula, not the regular stuff that expands with force and can bow frames) fills the cavity. One can runs $8–$12.

4. The mail slot, sidelights, and hardware#

A mail slot with a worn flap is a direct hole in your door. Replacement flaps cost $10–$20 and swap in minutes. Sidelight windows beside the door often leak at the glazing putty or the frame joint—caulk those seams. And check the lock and deadbolt cutouts; on some doors, air passes through the hardware bore. A small piece of foam weatherstripping behind the strike plate sometimes solves it.

The Step-by-Step Fix (90-Minute Weekend Job)#

  1. Diagnose first (20 min): incense test, dollar bill test, flashlight test. Write down every leak point.
  2. Measure the door (5 min): width, height, and the exact gap at bottom, top, and both sides at three points each. Note your threshold type—wood, aluminum, or none.
  3. Buy parts for your specific leaks (30 min at the store): budget $40–$80 total for a typical door (sweep or threshold, weatherstripping, caulk). Bring your measurements and a photo of the threshold.
  4. Clean all contact surfaces (10 min): alcohol on the door and frame where adhesive goes. Scrape old caulk and peeling weatherstripping with a putty knife.
  5. Install the threshold or sweep first (20–40 min): the bottom gap is the biggest leak; fix it before anything else.
  6. Install weatherstripping (20 min): work top-down, test the door’s swing after each section.
  7. Caulk the frame seams (10 min): interior and exterior. Smooth with a wet finger.
  8. Re-test (5 min): repeat the incense and dollar bill tests. You should feel resistance on the bill at every point.

What Most Guides Get Wrong#

They treat the draft snake as a solution. A fabric draft stopper blocks air only where it sits, does nothing for the sides and top, and gets kicked aside within a week. It’s a $15 placebo. It’s fine as a stopgap for a rental, but it is not sealing your door.

They ignore door sag and frame squareness. A door that’s dropped 1/4 inch at the latch side will never seal evenly with any weatherstripping—the gap is too uneven for one product to handle. Fix the sag first: tighten or replace the top hinge screws (longer 3-inch screws into the framing often do it), or shim the hinges. Sealing a crooked door is wasted money.

They recommend plastic window film for doors. Shrink film is for windows you don’t open. Sealing your primary entry door with film means you can’t use it, and it traps moisture against the door surface, which can damage paint and wood. Don’t do it.

The One Myth Worth Killing#

“New weatherstripping will fix my draft.” Weatherstripping seals the gap between door and frame. It does nothing for a gap under the door, a leak through the wall cavity, or a warped door that doesn’t sit flat. Most drafty front doors have at least two separate problems. Buying one product and hoping is why people re-do this job every year.

When to Stop DIYing#

If your door is visibly warped (hold a straightedge against it—more than 1/4 inch of bow is a problem), the frame is rotted at the sill, or you’ve sealed everything and still feel air, the door or frame needs replacement or professional repair. A pre-hung exterior door runs $300–$800 for the unit, plus $300–$600 for installation in most markets. That’s real money—but so is heating a house through a door that won’t seal, year after year.

One more number worth knowing: the DOE estimates air sealing and weatherstripping together can cut heating and cooling costs by around 10–20% for a typical home. On a $150/month heating bill, that’s $180–$360 a year—enough to pay back a $60 door-sealing job many times over. Do the diagnostic, buy the right parts, and fix the actual leaks. Your hand two inches from the door should feel nothing but still air.
