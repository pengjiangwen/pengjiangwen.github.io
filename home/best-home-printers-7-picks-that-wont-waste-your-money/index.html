Best Home Printers: 7 Picks That Won't Waste Your Money

Shopping for a home printer? Compare the best inkjet, laser, and tank models for documents, photos, and tight budgets—plus ink costs to watch.
September 27, 2026 · 5 min · 991 words · TechLife Guide

Introduction#

The last printer I bought came with a “starter” ink cartridge that died after 40 pages. Forty. The replacement set cost almost as much as the printer itself. That’s the dirty secret of the printer aisle: the cheap box on the shelf is rarely the cheap machine to own.

So before you buy, flip the math. A $79 inkjet with $65 cartridges is a worse deal than a $200 tank printer that runs for pennies a page. This guide breaks down the best home printers by what you actually print—school essays, tax forms, birthday photos, or all three—and flags the ink costs that manufacturers bury in the fine print.

First, Answer Three Questions#

Skip this and you’ll overpay. Every good printer recommendation starts here.

1. What do you print most? Text documents and shipping labels point to laser. Photos and color-heavy school projects point to inkjet. A true mix needs a tank inkjet or a color laser.

2. How often do you print? If it’s once a month, inkjet printheads can clog between jobs. Laser toner sits for months without drying out. Occasional printers should lean laser or a tank model with self-cleaning cycles.

3. What’s your real cost per page? Do the math before buying:

  • Standard inkjet cartridge: $30–$45 for 200–300 pages = roughly 12–18 cents per page
  • Tank inkjet: $13–$20 per bottle, yielding 3,000–7,500 pages = under 1 cent per page
  • Laser toner: $60–$90 for 1,200–3,000 pages = 3–7 cents per page

That gap adds up. Print 20 pages a week and a tank printer can save you $100+ per year over a cartridge inkjet.

Best Overall for Families: Tank Inkjet#

For most households juggling homework, recipes, and the occasional photo, a refillable tank inkjet is the sweet spot. Models like the Epson EcoTank and Canon MegaTank lines ship with two to three years of ink in the box. No chip-locked cartridges, no panic runs to the office supply store before a project is due.

Watch for: Print speed. Tank printers are slower than cartridge models, often 10–15 pages per minute in black. If you’re printing a 30-page packet, make coffee. Also check whether the model has a flatbed scanner—the cheapest tanks are print-only.

Best for: Families printing 50+ pages a month who want color without the cartridge tax.

Best for Documents Only: Monochrome Laser#

If 90% of your printing is text—forms, tickets, contracts, school essays—a black-and-white laser is the most reliable machine you can buy. They don’t clog. They spit out 30+ pages per minute. And third-party toner is cheap and plentiful.

A budget monochrome laser from Brother or HP typically runs $100–$150, and a high-yield toner cartridge can push 3,000 pages. That’s years of printing for most homes.

The catch: No color. If your kid needs a poster with colored headings, you’re out of luck. Some families solve this by pairing a cheap laser with a budget color inkjet for the rare color job.

Best for: Remote workers, students, and anyone who prints mostly black text.

Best for Photos: Dedicated Photo Inkjet#

Photo printers are a different animal. The good ones use six or more ink colors, including light cyan and light magenta, which smooth out skin tones and gradients that standard four-color printers turn grainy.

Expect to pay $200–$500, and expect ink to be expensive—photo printing always is. The trade-off is prints that actually look like photographs instead of faded office copies. If you only print a few 4x6 snapshots a month, consider a print service instead; the math rarely favors owning a photo printer for low volume.

Best for: Scrapbookers, hobbyist photographers, and anyone framing their own prints.

Best Budget Pick: Compact Inkjet Under $100#

Sometimes you just need a printer, cheap, today. A basic inkjet from Canon or HP will handle color documents and casual photos for under $100. Just go in with clear eyes: the included cartridges are half-empty, replacements are pricey, and you may spend more on ink in year one than on the printer.

Money-saving tip: Look for a model that accepts XL or high-yield cartridges. They cost more upfront but cut your cost per page by 30–50%. Also check whether your local library charges 10–15 cents per page—for very low volume, it’s genuinely cheaper to print there.

Features Worth Paying For (and Ones to Skip)#

Worth it:

  • Auto-duplex printing — prints both sides automatically, halving your paper use
  • Wi-Fi and mobile printing — AirPrint and Mopria let you print from your phone without a computer
  • A real flatbed scanner — the feed-style scanners on cheap all-in-ones mangle receipts and book pages

Skip it:

  • Fax capability — you haven’t faxed anything since 2009
  • Touchscreen displays — a $40 upcharge for a feature you’ll use twice
  • “Instant Ink”-style subscriptions — convenient, but you’re locked into the manufacturer’s pricing and page caps

Three Mistakes That Cost You Money#

Buying ink at the big-box store. Retail cartridges carry a huge markup. Ordering direct or from a reputable online seller often saves 20–30%.

Ignoring third-party ink (sometimes). For laser toner, reputable third-party brands are usually fine and cost half as much. For inkjet, results vary—clogging and printhead damage are real risks, so read recent reviews for your specific model.

Leaving the printer off for months. Inkjet printheads dry out and clog. Run a test page every two to three weeks, or use the printer’s built-in maintenance cycle. A clogged head can cost more to fix than the printer is worth.

The Bottom Line#

Match the printer to your actual habits, not the shelf display. Print mostly text? Buy a monochrome laser and stop thinking about it. Print a mix for a busy household? A tank inkjet pays for itself within a year. Print photos seriously? Budget for a six-color photo printer and accept that ink is the cost of the hobby.

Whatever you choose, calculate the cost per page before you swipe your card. The cheapest printer is almost never the cheapest to own.
