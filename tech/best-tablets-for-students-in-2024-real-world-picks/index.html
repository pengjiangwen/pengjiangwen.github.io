Best Tablets for Students in 2024: Real-World Picks

Drowning in tablet specs? I tested the top student tablets for note-taking, typing, and budget. Here are the only 4 worth buying this year.
August 17, 2026 · 8 min · 1610 words · TechLife Guide

Here are the only 4 worth buying this year. TAGS: student tablets, best tablet for college, note-taking tablet, iPad vs Samsung, budget tablet

Introduction#

Let’s be honest: buying a tablet for school is a trap. You walk in thinking you need a $1,200 iPad Pro, and you walk out with a stylus you never use and a keyboard that’s too small to type on. I’ve been there. I’ve also watched students cram for finals with a 10-inch slab that couldn’t handle split-screen PDFs, and I’ve seen others take notes on a $300 device that outlasted their laptop’s battery.

Here’s the truth: the best student tablet isn’t the one with the best specs—it’s the one that matches how you actually study. Are you a STEM major who needs to scribble equations? A humanities student writing 10-page essays? Or a commuter who needs something light for lecture halls?

I spent three weeks testing the current lineup across three price tiers. I took notes, typed essays, watched lectures, and drained batteries. Below are the only four tablets I’d recommend to a student in 2024—and one you should actively avoid.

The 3 Questions to Ask Before You Buy#

Before we get to the list, do a 60-second self-audit. This will save you from wasting money.

1. Do you need a keyboard, or just a pencil? If you’re writing essays, you need a keyboard. If you’re solving math problems, you need a stylus. Some tablets do both well (Samsung S9), some do one better than the other (iPad), and some do neither (most cheap Android tabs).

2. What does your school use? If your university is all-in on Google Docs, an Android tablet is fine. If you’re using Microsoft Office with heavy formatting, you need a tablet that handles Word without glitching—this rules out most budget options.

3. How long does your day run? If you’re on campus from 8 AM to 8 PM, battery life is non-negotiable. A tablet that dies at 4 PM is a paperweight.

The Winner for Most Students: iPad (10th Generation)#

Price: $349 (frequently on sale for $299) Best for: Note-taking, reading, general coursework, and students who already own an iPhone or MacBook.

The regular iPad is the default choice for a reason. It’s not flashy, but it hits the sweet spot of price, performance, and app support. The A14 Bionic chip is a few years old, but it still handles split-screen note-taking (GoodNotes + a PDF) without stuttering. I ran a 2-hour lecture recording while scribbling in Notability, and it didn’t drop a frame.

Why it beats the iPad Air: The Air costs $599 and offers a faster M1 chip and a laminated display. For a student, you won’t notice the difference unless you’re doing heavy video editing. Save the $250 and get the base model.

The catch: Apple’s first-gen Apple Pencil ($99) has a weird lighting connector that requires a dongle to charge. It’s clunky, but you only charge it once a week. The non-laminated screen means there’s a tiny gap between the glass and the display—you’ll hear a faint tap when writing. It doesn’t bother 90% of users, but try it in a store first.

Student tip: Buy the Logitech Combo Touch keyboard case ($149) instead of Apple’s Magic Keyboard. It’s $100 cheaper, has a trackpad, and offers better drop protection. Total cost: ~$450. That’s still less than a base iPad Air alone.

The Best for STEM and Art Students: Samsung Galaxy Tab S9#

Price: $799 (often $699 with student discount) Best for: Math, engineering, design, and anyone who wants a laptop replacement.

The Tab S9 is the only Android tablet I recommend without hesitation. It comes with the S-Pen included in the box—that’s a $129 value Apple makes you pay for. The stylus is the best in the business: it has 4,096 pressure levels and a rubber tip that feels like a real pen on paper. I used it to solve differential equations in Samsung Notes, and the handwriting-to-text conversion was scarily accurate.

Why it beats the iPad Pro for certain students: The S9’s AMOLED display is richer than the iPad’s LCD. If you’re looking at anatomy diagrams or color-coded circuit schematics, this matters. Plus, the S9 supports DeX mode—a desktop interface that lets you run multiple resizable windows. I plugged it into a monitor, connected a Bluetooth mouse, and wrote a 2,000-word essay in Google Docs without touching the screen.

The catch: Android tablets still have a weaker app ecosystem. Procreate (the gold standard for digital art) isn’t available. You’ll use Clip Studio Paint or Infinite Painter instead—both are good, but not identical. Also, the S9 is heavy (1.1 lbs). It’s fine for a backpack, but holding it one-handed for hours gets tiring.

Student tip: Samsung’s student discount portal usually knocks 10-15% off. Also, trade in any old tablet—even a broken one—for an extra $100 off. I got the S9 for $599 by trading in a dead Kindle Fire.

The Best Budget Pick: Lenovo Tab M11#

Price: $179 Best for: Reading textbooks, watching lectures, and light note-taking on a strict budget.

I didn’t want to like this tablet. It’s plastic, the screen is only 90Hz (which is fine, but not 120Hz), and the included stylus feels cheap. But for $179, it does 80% of what the iPad does.

The MediaTek Helio G88 processor is slow—apps take an extra second to open. But if you’re using it for one task at a time (read a PDF, then write a note, then watch a video), it’s perfectly fine. The battery lasted me 11 hours of mixed use, which is better than the iPad.

The catch: The stylus (Lenovo Pen) is passive, meaning it uses a battery-free EMR system. It works, but there’s no palm rejection. You’ll rest your hand on the screen and accidentally draw lines unless you wear a drawing glove. Also, the display is 1920x1200, which is sharp enough for text but not for tiny diagrams.

Who should buy this: If you’re a freshman taking gen-ed classes and you’re not sure if you’ll even use a tablet, start here. If you lose it or drop it, you’re out $180, not $800. I’d argue this is smarter than financing a flagship.

Student tip: Install a matte screen protector ($10). It reduces glare in bright lecture halls and gives the stylus more friction, making writing feel more natural.

The Best for Essay Writers: Microsoft Surface Pro 9#

Price: $999 (but often $799 with education discount) Best for: Students who need a full Windows laptop and a tablet in one.

The Surface Pro 9 is technically a 2-in-1 laptop, but it functions best as a tablet with a kickstand. For students who live in Word, Excel, and citation managers (Zotero, EndNote), this is the only device that runs the full desktop versions flawlessly. I’ve seen students try to format APA papers on an iPad, and it’s a nightmare. On the Surface, it’s just Windows.

The catch: The keyboard ($139) and Slim Pen 2 ($129) are sold separately. That brings the total to $1,267. That’s expensive. Also, battery life is mediocre—I got about 7 hours of real use, which is less than the iPad and Samsung.

Who should buy this: Only buy this if you don’t own a laptop. If you already have a MacBook or a Windows PC, don’t buy the Surface. Instead, get the iPad and use your laptop for heavy writing. The Surface is a compromise device—it replaces two things, but it’s not the best at either.

Student tip: Buy the previous year’s model (Surface Pro 8) refurbished. It’s the same chassis and screen, just with a slightly older Intel chip. You’ll save $300 and lose almost nothing.

The One to Avoid: Amazon Fire Max 11#

Price: $229 Why you shouldn’t buy it: Amazon’s tablets run a forked version of Android that doesn’t support the Google Play Store by default. You can sideload it, but it’s a hassle. More importantly, the stylus is sold separately ($34), and it’s inaccurate—I experienced a 2mm offset when writing. For note-taking, that’s unusable.

The only reason to buy this is if you exclusively consume content (Netflix, Kindle, YouTube) and never plan to write. But if that’s your use case, just use your phone.

Actionable Tips for Any Tablet You Buy#

1. Download your textbooks as PDFs before class. Don’t rely on school Wi-Fi. I’ve watched students panic because they couldn’t load a 500-page textbook in a basement lecture hall. Download everything you can the night before.

2. Use a cloud-syncing note app. GoodNotes (iOS) and Samsung Notes (Android) both sync to the cloud. If you drop your tablet, your notes survive. This is non-negotiable.

3. Buy a matte screen protector. I’ve said this twice already, but it’s that important. It reduces eye strain during long reading sessions and makes writing feel like paper.

4. Check your school’s IT department for free licenses. Many universities offer free Microsoft 365, Adobe, and note-taking apps. I saved $100/year by using my school’s portal instead of paying retail.

Final Verdict#

  • Most students: iPad 10th Gen + Logitech keyboard case.
  • STEM/Art students: Samsung Galaxy Tab S9 (the S-Pen included is the dealbreaker).
  • Strict budget: Lenovo Tab M11 (but buy a drawing glove).
  • Laptop replacement: Surface Pro 9 (only if you don’t own a laptop).

The tablet market is crowded, but these four cover 95% of student needs. Don’t overthink it. Pick the one that matches your major, not the one with the best marketing. Your bank account will thank you.
