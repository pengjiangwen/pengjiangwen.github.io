Smartwatch Buying Guide: What Actually Matters in 2024

Stop guessing which smartwatch to buy. This guide covers battery, health sensors, OS, and real-world use cases to help you choose wisely.

Introduction#

I’ve owned seven smartwatches over the past decade. My first was a clunky Pebble with a black-and-white e-paper screen that could barely tell the time without glitching. Last week, I tested a $900 Garmin Epix Pro alongside a $250 Pixel Watch 2. The gap in features has narrowed dramatically, but the confusion for buyers has only grown.

You don’t need to spend a grand to get a great smartwatch. But you also shouldn’t buy the cheapest option and hope it works for your lifestyle. The wrong smartwatch becomes a $300 notification buzzer that you stop charging after three months. The right one becomes a tool you wear daily for years.

This guide cuts through the marketing hype. We’ll look at battery life, operating systems, health sensors, screen types, and the one question most reviews ignore: What do you actually want this watch to do for you?

The One Question You Must Answer First#

Before you look at a single spec, ask yourself: Will I wear this watch to sleep and charge it daily, or do I want to leave it on for a week?

That single answer splits the entire market into two camps.

If you want sleep tracking, SpO2 monitoring overnight, and a watch that feels like part of your body, you need a device that lasts at least 3–5 days on a charge. The Apple Watch Ultra 2 gets about 36 hours with normal use. The Garmin Venu 3 gets 14 days. The difference isn’t small—it’s the difference between a bedside charging ritual and forgetting you’re wearing a watch.

If you plan to take it off every night anyway, battery life matters less. You can buy an Apple Watch Series 9 or a Samsung Galaxy Watch 6 and charge it while you shower. That’s fine.

But if you want to track a multi-day hike, monitor your sleep without interruption, or simply hate another device to charge, skip the “daily charge” watches entirely.

Operating Systems: The Ecosystem Trap#

Apple Watch (watchOS)#

If you own an iPhone, the Apple Watch is the obvious choice—but it’s also the only choice for full functionality. You cannot pair an Apple Watch with an Android phone. Period.

The trade-off is a polished, fluid experience. App selection is strong. Fall detection, crash detection, and ECG are well-tested. The new double-tap gesture (tap your index finger and thumb together) works surprisingly well for answering calls or snoozing alarms without touching the screen.

Best for: iPhone users who want seamless integration, strong health tracking, and a huge app ecosystem.

Wear OS (Google + Samsung)#

Wear OS 4, co-developed by Google and Samsung, has finally become a viable platform. The Pixel Watch 2 and Galaxy Watch 6 series run it well. You get Google Maps, Google Wallet, YouTube Music, and decent third-party app support.

The catch? Battery life still lags behind Garmin and Fitbit. Most Wear OS watches last 1–2 days. The Pixel Watch 2 gets about 24 hours with always-on display enabled. That’s better than previous generations, but still a daily charge.

Best for: Android users who want smart features (notifications, payments, apps) over long battery life.

Garmin / Fitbit / Amazfit (Proprietary OS)#

These watches run their own operating systems. They’re generally more focused on fitness and health than app integration.

Garmin’s OS is incredibly stable and battery-efficient. You can’t install many third-party apps, but you don’t need to—the built-in sports profiles, navigation, and training metrics are deeper than anything on Wear OS or watchOS.

Fitbit (now owned by Google) offers a middle ground. The Fitbit app is excellent for sleep and stress tracking, but the hardware has been stagnant. The Fitbit Sense 2 is fine, but the Pixel Watch 2 does everything better for the same price.

Amazfit watches are the budget champions. The Amazfit Balance costs $230, lasts 14 days, and tracks your heart rate, sleep, and workouts reasonably well. You lose the polish of an Apple Watch, but you gain battery life and save money.

Best for: Fitness-focused users, athletes, or anyone who values battery life over apps.

Health Sensors: What Actually Works#

Smartwatch health tracking has improved dramatically, but not all sensors are equal.

Heart Rate#

Optical heart rate sensors are now accurate enough for daily wear and steady-state exercise. During high-intensity intervals or weightlifting, they still lag behind a chest strap. If you’re a serious runner or cyclist, get a watch that supports external heart rate monitors via Bluetooth or ANT+ (Garmin and Polar do this; Apple and Samsung do not).

Blood Oxygen (SpO2)#

Nearly every watch now includes SpO2. It’s useful for altitude acclimation or sleep apnea screening, but don’t rely on it as a medical device. The readings can vary by 2–4% compared to a fingertip pulse oximeter.

ECG#

Apple, Samsung, and Garmin (on select models) offer single-lead ECG. If you have a known heart condition or family history of atrial fibrillation, this feature is genuinely valuable. If you’re a healthy 30-year-old, it’s a nice-to-have, not a dealbreaker.

Sleep Tracking#

This is the most inconsistent sensor across brands. Apple Watch sleep tracking is basic but reliable. Garmin’s advanced sleep tracking (Firstbeat Analytics) provides detailed sleep stages, body battery, and sleep score. Fitbit historically led the category, but recent models have been hit-or-miss.

Actionable tip: If sleep tracking matters to you, read independent reviews from The Quantified Scientist on YouTube. He tests watches against medical-grade devices and publishes the raw data.

Stress / Body Battery#

Garmin’s “Body Battery” and Fitbit’s “Stress Management Score” use heart rate variability (HRV) to estimate your physical readiness. These features are surprisingly useful for managing daily energy. I’ve found that ignoring my Body Battery and pushing through a workout often leads to poor performance or injury.

Screen Types: AMOLED vs. Memory-in-Pixel (MIP)#

This is a niche detail that most buyers overlook—and it matters more than resolution.

AMOLED (Apple Watch, Samsung, Pixel Watch, Garmin Venu, Fenix 7 AMOLED): Beautiful, vibrant colors, deep blacks, great indoors. But in direct sunlight, battery life drops sharply because the screen needs to crank up brightness. Always-on display mode drains battery faster.

Memory-in-Pixel (MIP) (Garmin Instinct, Fenix 7 Solar, Coros Vertix): Low-power screens that reflect ambient light. They’re always on, use almost no battery, and are perfectly readable in bright sun. The trade-off is a muted, grayish look indoors. No vibrant wallpapers or flashy watch faces.

Which to choose? If you spend weekends hiking, running trails, or working outdoors, get MIP. If you’re mostly in an office or gym, AMOLED looks better and works fine.

GPS and Navigation#

Every modern smartwatch has GPS. The difference is accuracy and battery drain.

  • Standard GPS (most watches): Good for city runs and walks. Loses track in dense forests or between tall buildings.
  • Multi-band GPS (Apple Watch Ultra, Garmin Fenix 7X, Coros Apex 2 Pro): Uses multiple satellite frequencies simultaneously. Much more accurate in challenging environments. Worth the premium if you run trails, hike, or live in a city with skyscrapers.
  • GPS endurance: Garmin watches can track a marathon with GPS and still have 50% battery left. An Apple Watch Ultra can handle a full marathon but might be low by the end. A standard Apple Watch Series 9 will struggle to last a half marathon with music playback.

Real example: I ran a 50K trail race with a Garmin Fenix 7S. After 6 hours of GPS tracking, it still had 40% battery. My friend’s Apple Watch Series 8 died at mile 22.

Durability and Design#

Water Resistance#

Most watches are 5 ATM (50 meters) rated, meaning they’re fine for swimming and showering. The Apple Watch Ultra and Garmin Descent series are rated to 100 meters.

Important: Don’t press buttons underwater on a non-dive watch. Water can seep in through the seals. Rinse your watch with fresh water after swimming in saltwater or chlorinated pools.

Scratch Resistance#

Sapphire crystal glass is standard on high-end Garmin and Apple Watch Ultra models. It’s nearly impossible to scratch. Gorilla Glass (used on most mid-range watches) scratches more easily. If you work with your hands or do outdoor activities, pay extra for sapphire.

Case Material#

Titanium is lighter and stronger than stainless steel. Aluminum is fine for casual use but dents easily. Plastic (Garmin Instinct) is light, cheap, and nearly indestructible—but looks cheap.

Actionable Buying Recommendations#

Under $250: Best Value#

  • Amazfit Balance ($230): 14-day battery, decent health tracking, AMOLED screen. The best budget smartwatch in 2024.
  • Garmin Forerunner 55 ($200): Excellent GPS, training features, and battery life. No music or maps, but perfect for runners on a budget.

$250–$500: The Sweet Spot#

  • Garmin Venu 3 ($450): AMOLED screen, 14-day battery, excellent sleep and stress tracking. The best all-rounder for most people.
  • Pixel Watch 2 ($350): Best Wear OS experience, great health sensors, comfortable design. Only downside is 24-hour battery.
  • Apple Watch Series 9 ($399): Best for iPhone users. Do everything well, but charge daily.

$500+: Premium#

  • Apple Watch Ultra 2 ($799): Titanium, multi-band GPS, 36-hour battery, action button. For serious outdoor enthusiasts who also want Apple ecosystem.
  • Garmin Fenix 7X Solar ($900): 37-day battery with solar, MIP screen, maps, multi-band GPS. Overkill for most people, but unmatched for adventurers.

Final Thoughts#

A smartwatch is a personal device. What works for a trail runner won’t work for a desk worker who wants notifications and sleep tracking.

My advice: Buy for the battery life you want, not the features you think you need. A watch with 30 features and 1-day battery will be abandoned after two weeks. A watch with 10 features and 7-day battery will become part of your routine.

Start with your phone ecosystem. Then decide if you want a daily charger or a week-long companion. Everything else—GPS accuracy, screen type, health sensors—comes second to that choice.

I still miss my Pebble sometimes. But my Garmin Venu 3 has stayed on my wrist for six months straight. That’s the real test.
