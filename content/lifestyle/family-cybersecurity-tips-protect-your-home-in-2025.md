## Introduction

Last Thanksgiving, my sister-in-law got a text from "FedEx" saying a package was held up at customs and needed a $2.99 redelivery fee. She paid it. Within an hour, someone had charged $4,000 to her credit card. The kicker? Her 11-year-old son had used her phone earlier that day to download a "free" Minecraft mod from a sketchy site. That's how the scammers got her number in the first place.

That story isn't unusual. According to the FBI's Internet Crime Report, Americans lost over $12.5 billion to cybercrime in 2023—and families are prime targets because we have more devices, more accounts, and more people sharing them. A household with two parents, three kids, a smart TV, a Ring doorbell, and four phones isn't a home anymore. It's a small network, and nobody's guarding it.

The good news: you don't need to be a tech expert to lock things down. You need a Saturday afternoon and a willingness to have a few awkward conversations. Here's how to do it.

## Start With the Weakest Link: Your Passwords

Most family breaches don't happen because of sophisticated hacking. They happen because someone used `Fluffy2019!` for their bank, their email, and their Disney+ account.

### The One Rule That Changes Everything

Use a password manager. Not "consider it." Use one. Bitwarden is free and works for the whole family. 1Password costs a few dollars a month and is easier for kids to understand. Either one will generate and store unique passwords for every account so you never have to remember them.

Set it up on a shared family computer first, then install the mobile apps. Yes, it takes an hour. Yes, it's worth it.

### Turn On Two-Factor Authentication Everywhere

Two-factor authentication (2FA) means that even if someone steals your password, they still can't get in without a second code sent to your phone or generated by an app. Turn it on for:

- Email accounts (these are the master keys to everything else)
- Banking and credit card apps
- Social media
- Any account tied to your kids' school

Use an authenticator app like Google Authenticator or Authy rather than SMS codes when possible. SMS can be intercepted through SIM-swapping attacks, which have hit families hard in recent years.

## Teach Kids What Phishing Actually Looks Like

Telling a 9-year-old "don't click suspicious links" is useless. They don't know what suspicious means. Show them.

### The Family Phishing Drill

Once a month, sit down with your kids and look at their email or messages together. Point out:

- Messages that create urgency ("Your account will be closed in 24 hours!")
- Senders with weird addresses (support@amaz0n-help.com)
- Links where the URL doesn't match the company name
- Anything asking for a password, gift card, or personal info

Make it a game. Whoever spots the fake message first gets to pick dinner. Kids who learn to spot scams at 10 will be far safer at 25.

### The Gift Card Scam

This one targets teens hard. Someone poses as a friend or a "brand" and asks them to buy a gift card and send the code. No legitimate business or friend will ever ask for payment in gift cards. Drill that into them.

## Secure the Devices You Already Own

You probably have more internet-connected devices than you think. Count them: phones, tablets, laptops, smart TVs, gaming consoles, baby monitors, thermostats, doorbells, robot vacuums. Every one is a potential door into your home network.

### Update Everything (Yes, Everything)

Software updates aren't just about new features—they patch security holes that criminals actively exploit. Turn on automatic updates for:

- Phones and computers
- Router firmware (check your router's app)
- Smart home devices
- Your car's infotainment system, if it connects to Wi-Fi

If a device hasn't received a security update in over two years, it's time to replace it or disconnect it from the internet.

### Lock Down Your Router

Your router is the front door to your entire home network. Three quick changes:

1. Change the default admin password (not the Wi-Fi password—the admin one)
2. Use WPA3 or WPA2 encryption
3. Create a separate guest network for smart devices and visitors

That last one matters. If your kid's friend brings over a laptop with malware, it hits the guest network, not your laptop with tax documents on it.

## Set Up a Family Digital Agreement

Rules work better when everyone agrees to them. Sit down as a family and write out something simple:

- No devices in bedrooms after 9 p.m. (this also improves sleep—a real bonus)
- Parents get to know all passwords for kids under 16
- Nobody shares personal info—full name, school, address, birthday—in games or chats
- If something weird happens online, tell a parent immediately without fear of punishment

That last point is critical. Kids who fear getting in trouble for a mistake will hide it, and hidden problems get worse. Make it clear that telling you about a scam is always the right move.

## Watch What You Share as a Family

Oversharing is the quiet risk. That back-to-school photo with your child's teacher's name, school name, and classroom number? That's a roadmap for a stranger. The vacation post that announces you're in Cancún for 10 days? That's an invitation to burglars.

### The 3-Question Filter

Before posting anything about your family, ask:

1. Does this reveal a location, routine, or schedule?
2. Does it include identifying details (school, team, full name)?
3. Would I be comfortable if a stranger saw this?

If any answer is yes, wait until you're home or crop out the details.

## Know the Signs of a Compromised Account

You can't fix what you don't notice. Watch for:

- Emails in your sent folder you didn't write
- Friends asking why you sent them a weird link
- Password reset emails you didn't request
- Unexpected charges, even small ones (scammers test with $1 charges first)

If you spot any of these, change your password immediately, enable 2FA, and check your account recovery settings—scammers often add their own backup email so they can get back in.

## The Bottom Line

Family cybersecurity isn't about buying the fanciest software or becoming paranoid. It's about a handful of habits: strong unique passwords, 2FA everywhere, updates on autopilot, honest conversations with your kids, and a shared understanding that the internet is a public place.

Start with one thing today. Set up a password manager. Turn on 2FA for your email. Have the gift card talk with your teenager. Small moves stack up, and the family that does them together is a much harder target than the one that doesn't.
