## Introduction

I've downloaded and deleted more learning apps than I care to admit. Duolingo streaks that died at 47 days. A coding app I opened twice. A "learn anything" platform that taught me mostly how to watch ads.

But a few apps stuck — and they stuck because they were genuinely good, not because they gamified me into submission. This list is the result: the free learning apps I've either used long-term myself or watched friends and family actually finish courses on. No affiliate fluff, no "top 50" padding. Just the ones worth your phone's storage.

One honest note before we start: "free" means different things. Some apps are fully free, some are free with ads, and some use a freemium model where the free tier is genuinely useful but the paid tier is better. I'll flag which is which so you're not surprised.

## Language Learning

### Duolingo

The obvious pick, and it earns the spot. Duolingo's free tier is generous — you get the full course tree, and ads are the only real tax. The gamification (streaks, leagues, hearts) is polarizing, but it works for building a daily habit.

**Where it's weak:** Speaking practice and real conversation. You can finish the Spanish tree and still freeze when someone asks you a question at normal speed. Use Duolingo as your daily warm-up, not your whole strategy.

**Pro tip:** Turn off leagues in settings if the competitive pressure makes you anxious rather than motivated. Your streak survives either way.

### Anki

Not flashy, not fun, and possibly the most powerful free learning tool on this list. Anki uses spaced repetition — it shows you flashcards right before you'd forget them. Medical students swear by it, but it works for anything: vocabulary, capitals, guitar chords, JavaScript syntax.

The catch: you build your own decks (or download shared ones), and the interface looks like it's from 2008. Push past that. Twenty minutes a day with Anki beats two hours of passive video watching.

## Coding and Tech Skills

### freeCodeCamp

This is the real deal. freeCodeCamp is a nonprofit offering thousands of hours of coding curriculum — web development, Python, data analysis, machine learning — completely free, no ads, no paywall. You earn certifications by building actual projects.

I've met people who landed junior developer jobs using freeCodeCamp as their primary resource. It's not a shortcut, but it's a legitimate path. Start with the Responsive Web Design certification; it's the gentlest on-ramp.

### SoloLearn

Better for bite-sized learning on your phone. SoloLearn's coding courses (Python, JavaScript, SQL, and more) are structured as short lessons with quizzes and a code playground. The free version covers a lot; the Pro tier adds more practice projects.

**Best use case:** commutes and waiting rooms. You're not going to become a developer through SoloLearn alone, but it's a solid way to test whether coding interests you before committing to something heavier.

## Math and Science

### Khan Academy

Still the gold standard for free education, and it's not close. Khan Academy covers math from counting to calculus, plus physics, chemistry, biology, economics, and test prep — all free, all ad-free, all aligned to real curricula.

The math courses are the standout. If you're relearning algebra as an adult, helping a kid with homework, or prepping for the SAT, this is where you start. Sal Khan's explanations are calm and clear, and the practice exercises actually adapt to your level.

### Brilliant (Free Tier)

Brilliant's interactive approach to math and science is genuinely engaging — you learn by solving puzzles, not watching lectures. The catch: the free tier is limited to a daily lesson or two. It's enough to sample the style, but if it clicks for you, you'll likely want the paid version.

Worth downloading to test-drive. Just know what you're getting.

## Music, Art, and Practical Skills

### Yousician (Free Tier)

Want to learn guitar, bass, ukulele, or piano? Yousician listens to you play through your phone's microphone and gives real-time feedback. The free tier gives you a limited daily practice window, but it's enough to build a foundation.

The feedback is the killer feature. Playing along to YouTube tutorials, you have no idea if you're in time. Yousician tells you.

### Sketchbook (by Autodesk)

A full-featured digital drawing app, free, no ads, no subscription. If you want to learn digital art on a tablet or phone, this removes the "I can't afford Procreate" excuse. The brush engine is surprisingly deep for a free app.

Pair it with free YouTube tutorials from artists like Proko or Draw with Jazza, and you've got a complete beginner's art course for zero dollars.

### Google's Applied Digital Skills

Less famous than it should be. Google offers free video-based courses on practical digital skills — spreadsheets, email etiquette, resume building, online safety, even basic data analysis. Each course is a series of short videos with hands-on activities.

**Who it's for:** anyone who feels behind on workplace tech, or students prepping for their first job. It's unglamorous and genuinely useful.

## Test Prep and Academic Subjects

### Quizlet

Flashcards, practice tests, and study sets for basically every subject and exam. The free tier covers flashcards and basic study modes; the paid tier adds more advanced features. Millions of study sets are already made by other users, so you can often find exactly what you need without building anything.

**Warning:** user-made sets vary wildly in quality. Check a set's reviews before trusting it for an exam.

### Coursera Audit Mode

Not technically an "app" in the casual sense, but Coursera's mobile app lets you audit thousands of university courses — from Yale, Stanford, Google, IBM — for free. You don't get the certificate, but you get the lectures, readings, and often the assignments.

If you want structured, university-level learning without paying tuition, audit mode is one of the best-kept secrets in online education.

### TED

Short talks, free, endlessly browsable. TED isn't a structured course, but it's excellent for exposure — 15 minutes on a topic you know nothing about, from someone who's spent years on it. Great for discovering what you actually want to learn next.

## How to Actually Stick With a Learning App

Downloading apps is easy. Using them past week two is the hard part. Three things that work:

**1. Attach it to an existing habit.** Not "I'll practice Spanish daily" — that's a wish. Instead: "I'll do one Duolingo lesson while my coffee brews." The habit you already have becomes the trigger.

**2. Pick one app, not five.** The biggest mistake I've made repeatedly. Five apps means five half-finished things. One app means actual progress.

**3. Set a floor, not a goal.** "Ten minutes minimum" beats "one hour a day" because you'll actually do it on bad days, and consistency matters more than intensity for learning.

## The Bottom Line

The best free learning app is the one you'll open tomorrow. freeCodeCamp and Khan Academy are the strongest overall picks for depth and quality. Duolingo and Anki win for daily habit-building. Yousician and Sketchbook prove that creative skills don't require a subscription.

Start with one. Give it two weeks. If it doesn't stick, swap it — that's not failure, that's calibration.
