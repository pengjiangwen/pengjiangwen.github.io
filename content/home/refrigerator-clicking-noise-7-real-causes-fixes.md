## Introduction

It's 11:47 p.m. and your fridge is clicking every 30 to 90 seconds like a metronome set to "annoying." You open the door, the light works, the milk is still cold—but that click keeps coming. Here's the number that matters: a failed start relay is the culprit in roughly half of all "refrigerator clicking but not cooling" service calls I've seen over 15 years of appliance repair, and the part costs $12 to $35. A compressor replacement, the thing that clicking often precedes, runs $450 to $900 installed. That gap is why you should read this before you call anyone.

Clicking isn't one problem. It's a symptom with at least seven distinct causes, and they range from "ignore it" to "unplug it now." Below is how to tell them apart in about ten minutes with your ears, a multimeter, and no guesswork.

## First: Where Is the Click Coming From?

Before you diagnose anything, localize the sound. Put your hand on the fridge and follow your ears.

- **Back/bottom rear panel:** The compressor and start relay live here. A click from this zone that repeats every minute or two and is followed by a hum—or silence—points to the start circuit.
- **Inside the fresh food compartment, upper rear:** That's the evaporator fan and air damper. A single click when the compressor cycles on or off is normal damper operation.
- **Behind the fridge, near the wall:** The condenser fan or the ice maker water valve.
- **From the ice maker itself:** A click every 2–3 hours during a harvest cycle is normal. A click every few minutes is not.

One rule of thumb: a click that occurs *once* per cooling cycle (every 30–60 minutes) is usually a relay or damper doing its job. A click that repeats every 30–90 seconds with no cooling in between is a start circuit failing to start the compressor. That's the pattern you need to act on.

## Cause 1: The Start Relay (Most Likely, Cheapest Fix)

The compressor doesn't start on its own. A small device—the start relay, sometimes called a PTC relay—gives it a jolt, then drops out of the circuit. When the relay's internal contacts wear out, it clicks as it tries to engage, the compressor hums for a few seconds, the overload protector trips, and the whole sequence repeats. Click. Hum. Silence. Click.

**How to confirm:** Unplug the fridge. On most Whirlpool, Frigidaire, and GE top-freezer models, the relay is a black plastic box clipped to the left side of the compressor can at the back. Pull it off (it unplugs from the compressor pins). Shake it. If it rattles like a baby toy, the contacts or the PTC disc inside have disintegrated—replace it.

**Cost and time:** $12–$35 for the part (search your model number plus "start relay" on RepairClinic or Amazon). Twenty minutes with a nut driver. Match the part number printed on the old relay, not just the fridge model—relay revisions change.

**One caveat:** On newer inverter-driven compressors (common in LG and Samsung French-door models made after roughly 2015), there is no traditional start relay. Those use a control board and a different starting method. If your fridge has an inverter compressor, skip to Cause 4.

## Cause 2: The Overload Protector

Bolted or clipped next to the relay, the overload protector is a thermal switch that cuts power when the compressor draws too much current. It clicks when it trips—and it trips when the compressor is working too hard or the relay has failed. Often you replace the relay and the overload together as a $25 kit, because a failing relay stresses the overload and vice versa.

If the overload clicks but the compressor can is *hot to the touch* (over 140°F), stop. That's not a relay problem. That's a compressor or refrigerant problem, and the next section matters.

## Cause 3: A Dying Compressor (The Expensive One)

Here's the test that separates a $25 fix from a $700 one. Unplug the fridge, remove the relay, and check the compressor's three pins with a multimeter set to resistance. Between any two pins you should read roughly 1–10 ohms; between any pin and the copper tubing (ground), you should read infinite resistance. If you read 0 ohms to ground, the windings are shorted—the compressor is dead and no relay will save it.

A compressor that clicks, hums for 5–10 seconds, trips the overload, and gets scorching hot is also on its way out. Compressor replacement runs $450–$900 installed, and on a fridge older than 10–12 years, that's usually more than the appliance is worth. Put the money toward a new unit.

## Cause 4: The Control Board

Modern fridges—especially Samsung, LG, and GE models with digital displays—route compressor and fan commands through a main control board, typically behind a plastic cover at the back or inside the top of the fresh food section. A failing relay on the board can produce a sharp click every few seconds, sometimes accompanied by a flickering interior light or a display that resets.

**Tell-tale sign:** the click happens even when the fridge is in defrost mode or the door has been open for a while (compressor should be off). Boards run $120–$300. Before buying one, check for burnt solder joints or a bulging capacitor—a $2 capacitor and 30 minutes with a soldering iron fixes a surprising number of these.

## Cause 5: The Defrost Timer or Bi-Metal Thermostat

Older fridges (roughly pre-2005, and many Kenmore and Whirlpool models) use a mechanical defrost timer that advances one click every 8–12 hours and then runs a 20–30-minute defrost heater cycle. If you hear a single click followed by a faint sizzle or drip, that's normal.

The failure mode: the timer sticks, the fridge never enters defrost, and frost builds on the evaporator until the fridge stops cooling. You'll hear clicking as the timer tries to advance. The timer is a $15–$40 part behind the kick plate or in the control housing. The bi-metal defrost thermostat, a $10 part clipped to the evaporator coil, can also click as it opens and closes—if it fails open, you get a frozen coil and a warm fridge.

## Cause 6: The Evaporator or Condenser Fan

A fan with a bad bearing or a blade striking ice doesn't click so much as tick or rattle—but people describe it as clicking. Open the freezer, press the door switch, and listen. If the noise stops when the fan stops, you've found it.

- **Evaporator fan (inside, behind a panel):** $20–$60. Ice buildup striking the blade means a defrost problem, not just a fan.
- **Condenser fan (behind, near the floor):** $25–$70. Pull the fridge out and vacuum the coils—a fan laboring against a mat of pet hair draws more current and can cause relay chatter.

## Cause 7: The Ice Maker and Water Valve

An ice maker clicks when the mold thermostat calls for a harvest, when the ejector arms stall, or when the water inlet valve solenoids actuate. Normal harvest: a few clicks every 2–3 hours, then ice drops.

Abnormal: clicking every few minutes with no ice production. That's usually a stalled ejector motor ($40–$80 module) or a failed water valve ($25–$50). Turn the ice maker off at the bail arm or the control panel and see if the clicking stops. If it does, you've isolated it in one step.

## The 10-Minute Diagnostic Checklist

Work through this in order. Unplug the fridge before touching anything at the back.

1. **Localize the sound.** Rear-bottom, interior-upper, or ice maker? Write it down.
2. **Time the clicks.** Once per 30–60 minutes = likely normal cycling. Every 30–90 seconds = start circuit.
3. **Check cooling.** Put a thermometer in the fridge. Above 40°F in the main compartment after 24 hours means the compressor isn't running.
4. **Listen at the back.** Click, then hum, then silence? Start relay or compressor.
5. **Unplug and remove the start relay.** Shake it. Rattle = replace ($12–$35).
6. **Test compressor pins with a multimeter.** 1–10 ohms between pins, infinite to ground = compressor OK.
7. **Feel the compressor can.** Warm is fine. Too hot to hold your hand on for 2 seconds = compressor or refrigerant issue, call a tech.
8. **Turn off the ice maker.** Clicks stop? Ice maker or water valve.
9. **Press the door switch and listen for fans.** Fan noise = fan motor or ice strike.
10. **Vacuum the condenser coils.** Five minutes, free, and it prevents a whole category of overload trips.

If steps 1–6 point to the relay and the compressor tests fine, you can fix this yourself for under $40. If step 7 fails, stop and call a technician—refrigerant work requires a license and equipment you don't have.

## What Most Guides Get Wrong

**They tell you to replace the relay without testing the compressor.** A new relay on a shorted compressor clicks right back into failure within a day. Test the windings first. It's four minutes with a $20 multimeter and saves you a return shipping label.

**They call every click "normal."** A single click per cycle is normal. A click every 90 seconds with a warm fridge is a machine telling you the compressor won't start. Treating that as normal is how people lose $300 of frozen food over a holiday weekend.

**They ignore inverter compressors entirely.** Half the clicking-fridge advice online assumes a PTC relay that LG and Samsung inverter models don't have. If your fridge was built after about 2015 and has a linear or inverter compressor, the diagnosis path runs through the control board, not the relay.

## When to Stop DIY and Call a Tech

Call a professional if: the compressor can is too hot to touch, you read 0 ohms to ground on the compressor pins, you smell refrigerant or see oily residue on the lines, the control board shows burn marks you're not comfortable soldering, or the fridge is under a manufacturer warranty (Sears, LG, and Samsung commonly cover the sealed system for 5–10 years—opening it yourself voids that).

Expect a $100–$150 diagnostic fee, often credited toward the repair. A relay replacement by a tech runs $150–$250 total; a compressor, $450–$900. Get the diagnosis in writing before authorizing anything.

## The Bottom Line

Count the clicks before you panic. One click per cooling cycle is your fridge working. A click every minute with a warm fridge is a $20 relay nine times out of ten—and a compressor the tenth. Test before you replace, vacuum your coils twice a year, and know that the most expensive mistake here isn't the part. It's waiting three days on a clicking fridge while $250 of food quietly thaws.
