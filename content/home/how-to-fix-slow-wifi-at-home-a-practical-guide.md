## Introduction

You know the feeling. You're halfway through a movie, the buffer wheel starts spinning, and suddenly your smart TV is displaying a pixelated mess. Or worse — you're on a video call for work, and your voice starts sounding like a robot underwater.

Last month, my neighbor knocked on my door asking why her WiFi was "broken." She'd already called her internet provider twice. They told her everything looked fine on their end. Twenty minutes in her apartment, I found the culprit: her router was sitting inside a closed wooden cabinet, next to a microwave, behind a fish tank. Three simple changes later, her speed test went from 8 Mbps to 220 Mbps.

Most slow WiFi problems aren't actually problems with your internet provider. They're problems inside your home. Here's how to find and fix them.

## First, Run a Proper Speed Test

Before you change anything, you need a baseline. Go to a speed test site or app and run a test on a device connected directly to your router with an Ethernet cable, if possible. Then run the same test on WiFi from the same spot.

If the wired speed is fast and WiFi is slow, the problem is your wireless setup — not your internet plan. That's good news, because it's usually fixable for free.

Test in multiple rooms too. If one room is slow and another is fine, you've got a coverage problem, not a speed problem. Those require different solutions.

## Router Placement: The Most Overlooked Fix

Your router's location matters more than almost anything else. WiFi signals are radio waves, and radio waves don't like obstacles.

**Avoid these placements:**
- Inside cabinets, closets, or behind furniture
- Next to microwaves, cordless phones, or baby monitors (these interfere with the 2.4 GHz band)
- On the floor or in a basement corner
- Near fish tanks or large metal objects

**Better placement:**
- Central location in your home
- Elevated — on a shelf or mounted on a wall
- Out in the open with clear space around it

Think of it like a light bulb. Where would you put a lamp if you wanted to light up your whole house? That's roughly where your router should go.

## Switch to the 5 GHz Band (If You Haven't Already)

Most modern routers broadcast two networks: 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band travels farther but is slower and more crowded. The 5 GHz band is much faster but has shorter range.

If your devices are close to the router, connect them to the 5 GHz network. You'll often see an immediate, dramatic speed increase. Streaming devices, gaming consoles, and laptops in the same room as the router should almost always be on 5 GHz.

If your router uses "band steering" (one network name for both bands), it may be putting your devices on the wrong band. Check your router settings to see if you can split them into separate names.

## Update Your Router's Firmware

Router manufacturers regularly release firmware updates that fix bugs, improve performance, and patch security holes. Many people never update their router, running firmware that's years out of date.

Log into your router's admin panel (usually by typing something like 192.168.1.1 into your browser), find the firmware or update section, and check for updates. Some routers do this automatically — make sure that setting is turned on.

## Change Your WiFi Channel

In apartment buildings and dense neighborhoods, everyone's WiFi is fighting for the same channels. It's like a crowded highway with too many cars.

For the 2.4 GHz band, only channels 1, 6, and 11 don't overlap with each other. If your neighbors are all on channel 6, switching to 1 or 11 can help.

You can scan nearby networks using a free app like WiFi Analyzer (Android) or the built-in tools on most computers. Look for the least crowded channel and switch to it in your router settings.

On 5 GHz, this is less of an issue since there are far more channels available.

## Reduce Interference and Clutter

Every device in your home that emits radio waves is potentially interfering with your WiFi. That includes:

- Bluetooth speakers and headphones
- Wireless security cameras
- Smart home devices
- Older cordless phones

You don't need to get rid of these, but keep them away from your router. A few feet of distance can make a real difference.

## Consider a Mesh System or Extender

If your home is large, has thick walls, or has multiple floors, a single router may simply not be enough. This is where mesh WiFi systems shine.

A mesh system uses multiple units placed around your home that work together to create one seamless network. Unlike old-school extenders, mesh systems don't require you to manually switch networks as you move around.

They're not cheap — expect to spend $150 to $400 for a good system — but for larger homes, they're often the single most effective upgrade you can make.

If your budget is tight, a traditional WiFi extender can help, but placement is critical. Put it roughly halfway between your router and the dead zone, not in the dead zone itself.

## Check What's Eating Your Bandwidth

Sometimes your WiFi isn't slow — it's just being used up. Common bandwidth hogs include:

- 4K video streaming (uses up to 25 Mbps per stream)
- Cloud backups running in the background
- Large game downloads
- Someone else's video calls

Log into your router and look at connected devices. You might find a neighbor using your network (change that password), or a forgotten device downloading updates.

## When to Call Your Provider

If you've tried everything above and your wired speed is also slow, the problem is likely on your provider's end. Call them with your speed test results ready. Be specific: "I'm paying for 300 Mbps and getting 40 Mbps on a wired connection."

That's a much more effective conversation than "my internet is slow."

## A Quick Checklist

Before you spend money on new gear, run through this list:

1. Move your router to a central, open, elevated spot
2. Connect nearby devices to the 5 GHz band
3. Update your router's firmware
4. Change your WiFi channel
5. Move interfering devices away from the router
6. Check for bandwidth hogs on your network
7. Test again

Most people find that steps 1 through 3 solve the majority of their problems. If they don't, you'll at least know exactly what's wrong — and that makes it much easier to fix.
