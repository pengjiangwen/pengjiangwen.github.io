## Introduction

A normal load of towels should come out dry in 45 to 60 minutes in a vented electric dryer. If yours is running 90 minutes and the clothes still feel damp, something in the machine's air path, heat source, or load dynamics has changed. The dryer didn't "get old" — a specific component or habit is costing you time and money.

Here's the number that matters: a dryer with a 50% blocked vent doesn't just dry twice as slow. It runs hotter, trips the thermal fuse more often, and can add $30–$60 a year to your electric bill. Below is a practical diagnostic sequence, the most common causes ranked by likelihood, and the mistakes that send people shopping for a new appliance they don't need.

## Start With the Vent, Not the Dryer

Nine times out of ten, a slow dryer is a venting problem. The dryer pushes 100–200 cubic feet of moist, lint-laden air per minute through a 4-inch duct. Any restriction in that path backs everything up.

### The 10-minute vent check

1. **Unplug the dryer** (or kill the breaker for gas models — you're working near a gas line).
2. **Pull the dryer out** and disconnect the flexible duct from the back.
3. **Look inside the dryer's exhaust port** with a flashlight. A thin film of lint is normal. A felt-like mat is a blockage.
4. **Reach into the wall duct** as far as your arm goes and sweep out lint.
5. **Run the dryer on air-fluff for 5 minutes** with the duct disconnected. Hold your hand at the exhaust. Strong, warm airflow means the dryer's blower is fine and the problem is downstream.
6. **Inspect the outdoor vent hood.** The flap should swing freely. Bird nests, crushed flaps, and lint-packed screens are extremely common — I've pulled fist-sized nests out of hoods on second-floor walls.

If airflow at the dryer is strong but weak at the outside hood, the run itself is clogged. A 4-inch duct should have no more than 25 feet of total equivalent length, and every 90-degree elbow counts as 5 feet. Two elbows plus a 15-foot run puts you at 25 feet — right at the limit. Rigid metal duct beats flexible foil every time; the corrugated stuff traps lint in its ridges.

### The hidden killer: crushed flex duct

That accordion-style foil or plastic duct behind the dryer is the single most common cause I see. People shove the dryer back against the wall and crush the duct into an S-curve. A crushed section cuts airflow by half or more. Replace it with semi-rigid or rigid 4-inch duct, keep the run as short and straight as possible, and leave at least 4 inches of clearance behind the unit.

## When the Vent Is Clean but the Dryer Still Crawls

If airflow checks out, move to heat and mechanical causes. Work through these in order — each takes under 15 minutes.

### 1. The heating element or igniter

An electric dryer typically has a 4,000–5,600 watt heating element. If half the coil breaks, the dryer still tumbles and blows air, but the air is only warm, not hot. A load that used to take 50 minutes now takes 90. Replacement elements run $25–$60 and are a genuine DIY job on most Whirlpool, Amana, and Roper models — two screws and a wire harness.

Gas dryers use a glow-bar igniter ($20–$40) and a pair of solenoids on the gas valve ($15–$30 for the pair). A weak solenoid often works when cold and fails when hot, which is why some gas dryers dry one load fine and quit heating on the second.

### 2. The thermal fuse and cycling thermostat

A blown thermal fuse kills heat entirely — the drum tumbles on cold air. But a failing cycling thermostat can cause the opposite problem: it cuts heat too early, so the drum never reaches full temperature. Both parts cost under $25. Test with a multimeter set to continuity; a good fuse reads near zero ohms.

### 3. A clogged lint filter housing (not just the screen)

Everyone cleans the screen. Almost nobody cleans the housing behind it. On many Whirlpool and Maytag front-load-matched dryers, lint bypasses a torn screen and packs into the blower wheel housing. Pull the lint screen and shine a light down the slot — if you see a gray felt wall, vacuum it out with a crevice tool. On some models you can remove the front lower panel (four screws) and vacuum the blower wheel directly.

### 4. Moisture sensor bars

Sensor-dry mode relies on two metal bars inside the drum. Fabric softener residue coats them with a waxy film, so the dryer thinks clothes are still wet and runs the full timed cycle — or the reverse, shutting off early. Scrub the bars with a paper towel and a splash of white vinegar. This one fix solves a surprising number of "my dryer runs forever on sensor mode" complaints, especially on LG and Samsung models.

### 5. The load itself

A full drum of heavy denim and bath towels holds roughly 2–3 gallons of water after a high-spin wash. An overstuffed drum can't tumble, so hot air channels around the clothes instead of through them. Fill the drum no more than two-thirds full, and don't pack it — clothes need room to fall.

## What Most Guides Get Wrong

**Myth 1: "Add a wet towel to speed things up."** This advice floats around forums and it's backwards for most modern dryers. Adding moisture can trick old sensor systems, but on a dryer with a working sensor it just adds water to remove. Skip it.

**Myth 2: "Higher heat always dries faster."** High heat sets wrinkles, shrinks cotton, and on a partially blocked vent it's a fire risk. Medium heat with good airflow beats high heat with restricted airflow every time. If your dryer only performs on high, that's a vent problem wearing a disguise.

**Myth 3: "The dryer is dying, replace it."** A dryer is one of the simplest appliances you own — a motor, a heater, a thermostat, and a fuse. Most slow-dry complaints trace to a $15 part or a clogged duct. A repair tech charges $100–$150 for a service call; the parts are usually under $40. Unless the drum bearing is shot or the control board is dead (a $200–$350 part), repair is almost always the better call.

## A Quick Diagnostic Checklist

Run through this in order before you call anyone:

- [ ] Lint screen cleaned before every load
- [ ] Lint screen rinsed under water — if water beads up, the screen is coated and needs a soapy wash and dry
- [ ] Duct disconnected and checked for crushed sections
- [ ] Outdoor vent hood flap opens freely when the dryer runs
- [ ] Dryer run on air-fluff with duct off — strong airflow confirmed
- [ ] Heating element or igniter visually inspected for breaks
- [ ] Thermal fuse tested for continuity
- [ ] Sensor bars wiped with vinegar
- [ ] Load size at two-thirds capacity or less
- [ ] Dryer leveled — a tilted drum lets clothes bunch on one side

If you get through all ten and the dryer still takes 90 minutes, the blower wheel itself may be stripped or clogged. That's a $20–$40 part but requires removing the front panel and drum on most models — a two-hour job with a putty knife and a nut driver.

## The Bottom Line on Time and Money

A dryer that runs 90 minutes instead of 50 wastes about 2.5 kWh per load on an electric model. At 14 cents per kWh, that's roughly 35 cents a load — about $100 a year for a family doing eight loads a week. The vent cleaning takes 20 minutes and costs nothing. The thermal fuse costs $12. Start there.

One more thing: if your dryer is vented into a plastic flex line that snakes more than 8 feet, replace it this weekend. It's the cheapest insurance against both slow drying and the far more serious problem of lint ignition. Rigid 4-inch metal duct, short run, clean hood. That's the whole secret.
