## Introduction

A few years ago, a family in my neighborhood noticed their smart TV buffering constantly. Odd, but not alarming—until the internet provider called. Someone had been using their network to run a small file-sharing operation, and it had been going on for months. The router password? Still the default one printed on the sticker.

That story isn't unusual. Your home network is the front door to every device you own: laptops with tax documents, phones with banking apps, cameras pointed at your living room. Most people lock that door with a password they've never changed. Here's how to actually secure it, without needing a degree in computer science.

## Start With the Router (It's the Weakest Link)

### Change the Default Admin Credentials

Every router ships with a username and password like "admin/admin." These are publicly documented. If you've never logged into your router, do it today—usually by typing 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1 into a browser. Change both the admin username and password to something unique.

### Update Firmware Regularly

Router manufacturers patch security holes through firmware updates, but they can't force you to install them. Log into your router's admin panel and check for updates every few months. Some newer routers have an auto-update option—turn it on. If your router hasn't received a firmware update in over two years, it's probably time for a new one.

### Disable WPS

Wi-Fi Protected Setup lets you connect devices by pressing a button, which sounds convenient until you learn that its PIN method can be brute-forced in hours. Turn WPS off in your router settings. Connecting a new device manually takes thirty extra seconds.

## Lock Down Your Wi-Fi

### Use WPA3 (or WPA2 if You Must)

Check your wireless security setting. If it says WEP or "open," you're essentially broadcasting your traffic to the street. WPA3 is the current standard; WPA2-AES is acceptable if your devices don't support WPA3. Anything older should be replaced.

### Make Your Password Long, Not Clever

"Fluffy2019!" is not a strong password—it's a guessable one. Aim for at least 16 characters, mixing words in a way that's memorable to you but not obvious: something like "purplecouch7lampmonday." Length beats complexity every time. And never reuse your Wi-Fi password for other accounts.

### Hide Your Network Name? Skip It

Hiding your SSID (network name) sounds stealthy, but it doesn't stop determined attackers and it breaks your own devices' ability to reconnect smoothly. A strong password does far more for you.

## Segment Your Network

### Set Up a Guest Network

Your guest Wi-Fi isn't just for visitors. Put smart bulbs, TVs, and other IoT gadgets on it. These devices often have weak security and rarely get updates. Keeping them on a separate network means a compromised smart plug can't reach your laptop.

### Consider a VLAN If You're Technical

More advanced routers let you create VLANs—virtual networks that isolate device groups. It's overkill for most homes, but if you work from home with sensitive data, it's worth exploring.

## Pay Attention to the Devices Themselves

### Change Default Passwords on Everything

That IP camera with the password "12345"? It's a known entry point. Every connected device—cameras, printers, smart speakers—should have its default login changed the day you set it up.

### Enable Automatic Updates

Phones, computers, and even smart TVs push security patches. Turning on automatic updates means you don't have to think about it. For devices that don't support auto-updates, set a calendar reminder to check quarterly.

### Turn Off Features You Don't Use

Remote access, UPnP, and file sharing are convenient until they're not. If you don't use your router's remote management feature, disable it. UPnP, in particular, lets apps open ports without asking—a favorite trick of malware.

## Monitor and Maintain

### Check Who's on Your Network

Most routers list connected devices in the admin panel. Scan it occasionally. If you see a device you don't recognize, investigate immediately and change your Wi-Fi password. Some routers and apps (like Fing) will alert you to new devices automatically.

### Watch for Warning Signs

Sluggish internet, unexpected reboots, or a router admin page that won't load properly can all signal trouble. Don't dismiss them.

### Back Up Your Router Configuration

Once you've got everything set up the way you like, export the router's configuration file. If you ever need to factory reset, you can restore your settings in minutes instead of redoing everything.

## A Quick Reality Check

You don't need to do all twelve of these today. But pick three—changing your router password, updating firmware, and setting up a guest network—and you'll be ahead of most households. The goal isn't perfection; it's making your network annoying enough to break into that intruders move on to an easier target.

Your home network protects more than Wi-Fi. Treat it like it matters, and it'll return the favor.
