## Introduction

When my neighbor Dave got his first post-solar electric bill, he texted me a photo of it like it was a newborn baby. Eleven dollars. His previous August bill had been $214. That was three years ago, and he still brings it up at every barbecue.

But here's the part Dave doesn't mention as often: the two years he spent researching, the three installers who tried to oversell him, and the tree he had to cut down first. Solar isn't complicated, but it's full of small decisions that determine whether you save $30,000 over 25 years or wonder why your panels aren't producing what the salesperson promised.

This guide covers what actually matters when you're installing solar on a home — the real process, the real costs, and the questions most people don't think to ask until it's too late.

## How Home Solar Actually Works

Before you talk to anyone, understand the basic system. A typical home solar setup has four parts:

**Solar panels** capture sunlight and convert it to direct current (DC) electricity. **An inverter** converts that DC power into alternating current (AC), which your home and the grid use. **Mounting hardware** attaches everything to your roof (or ground). **Monitoring equipment** tracks production, usually through a phone app.

Most homes also connect to the utility grid. When your panels produce more than you use, the excess flows to the grid and you get credited for it — a process called net metering. Policies vary wildly by state and even by utility company, so this single detail can change your entire financial picture.

A standard home system runs between 6 and 10 kilowatts, which is roughly 15 to 25 panels. That's enough to cover most or all of a typical household's electricity use.

## The Installation Process, Step by Step

### Step 1: Energy Audit and Usage Review

Any reputable installer starts by pulling your last 12 months of electric bills. Not three months — twelve. Why? Because a system sized on summer usage will overproduce in winter, and one sized on winter usage will leave you short every July.

**Actionable tip:** Before your first consultation, log into your utility account and download your full usage history. If an installer doesn't ask for it, that's a red flag.

### Step 2: Site Assessment

Someone walks your property and evaluates:

- **Roof condition and age.** If your roof has fewer than 10 years of life left, replace it first. Removing and reinstalling panels later costs $2,000–$5,000.
- **Shading.** A single tree casting shade on one panel can drag down an entire string of panels unless you use microinverters or power optimizers.
- **Roof orientation and pitch.** South-facing is ideal in the Northern Hemisphere, but east-west systems still produce 80–85% of peak output.
- **Structural integrity.** Older homes may need reinforcement.

### Step 3: System Design and Permitting

The installer designs your layout, then handles permits with your city and utility. This step takes the longest — anywhere from two weeks to three months depending on your jurisdiction. You're not doing the paperwork, but you are waiting on it.

### Step 4: Installation Day

A typical residential install takes one to three days. Crews mount the racking, set the panels, run conduit, and wire the inverter. Expect some noise and a crew on your roof, but nothing dramatic.

### Step 5: Inspection and Interconnection

Your local building department inspects the work, then your utility approves the connection and swaps your meter. Only after this can you actually turn the system on.

## What Solar Really Costs in 2024

The national average for a residential system sits around **$2.50 to $3.50 per watt** before incentives. For a 7 kW system, that's roughly $17,500 to $24,500.

Then the federal Residential Clean Energy Credit knocks **30% off** the total cost. On a $20,000 system, that's a $6,000 credit. Many states add their own incentives on top.

But the sticker price isn't the number that matters. What matters is **payback period** — how long until your energy savings equal your upfront cost. In Hawaii or Massachusetts, that can be 5–7 years. In parts of the Southeast with cheap electricity and weak net metering, it can stretch past 15.

Run the numbers for your specific zip code before getting emotionally attached to the idea. Tools like EnergySage and the DOE's PVWatts calculator give you honest estimates.

## Buying vs. Leasing: The Decision That Haunts People

Solar leases and power purchase agreements (PPAs) let you skip the upfront cost. The company owns the panels; you pay a monthly rate for the power.

Sounds fine until you sell your house. Buyers have to agree to take over the lease, and if they don't qualify or don't want to, you may have to buy out the contract — sometimes for more than the system is worth.

**My honest take:** If you can afford to buy, buy. If you can't, look hard at whether a lease makes sense for your situation. Read every line of the escalator clause, which determines how much your payment rises each year.

## Mistakes That Cost Homeowners Real Money

**Skipping multiple quotes.** Get at least three. Prices for identical systems vary by 30–50% in the same market.

**Ignoring the inverter choice.** String inverters are cheaper but less efficient with shading. Microinverters cost more but optimize each panel individually. Ask which one you're getting and why.

**Forgetting about insurance.** Tell your homeowner's insurance company. Most policies cover rooftop solar, but you want it documented.

**Not checking the warranty details.** Panel warranties often run 25 years, but inverter warranties may only be 10–12. Labor warranties vary from 1 year to 10. Get it in writing.

**Assuming your roof is fine.** Have a roofer inspect it independently before installation. Installers aren't roofers.

## Maintenance: Less Than You Think

Solar panels have no moving parts. Rain handles most of the cleaning. You'll want to hose them down once or twice a year if you live in a dusty area or get heavy pollen.

The inverter is the component most likely to fail. Most last 10–15 years and cost $1,000–$2,000 to replace. Budget for it.

Beyond that, check your monitoring app occasionally. If production drops noticeably, something's wrong — a tripped breaker, a failed optimizer, or shade from a new tree branch.

## The Bottom Line

Solar pays off best when you treat it like a home improvement project, not a purchase from a salesperson. Get your usage data, understand your net metering policy, compare at least three quotes, and buy rather than lease if your finances allow.

Dave's $11 bill is real. So is the homework that got him there. Do yours, and you'll likely be the one texting photos at the next neighborhood cookout.
