## Introduction

In 2019, fewer than 1% of U.S. medical visits happened over video. By 2021, that figure had jumped to roughly 13% — and it has since settled into a durable 10–12% range, according to national claims data. That is not a pandemic blip. It is a permanent shift in how medicine gets delivered, and it has real consequences for your care.

Here is the problem: most articles about telehealth pros and cons read like a brochure. They list "convenient" on one side and "no physical exam" on the other and call it a day. That tells you nothing about when to book a video visit and when to get in the car.

This guide is different. It covers what telehealth genuinely does well, where it quietly fails, and a practical decision checklist you can run through in 30 seconds before your next appointment.

## What Telehealth Actually Includes (A Quick Clarification)

People use "telehealth" and "telemedicine" interchangeably, but they are not identical.

- **Telemedicine** is clinical care delivered remotely: a video visit with your doctor, a remote psychiatric evaluation, a dermatologist reviewing a photo of a mole.
- **Telehealth** is broader. It includes telemedicine plus things like remote patient monitoring, nurse triage lines, prescription management portals, and asynchronous messaging with your care team.

This matters because the pros and cons differ depending on which one you are using. A 10-minute video call for a refill has almost no downsides. A remote monitoring program for heart failure requires much more scrutiny.

## The Real Advantages of Telehealth

### 1. Access, Especially Where It's Scarce

Roughly 80% of rural counties in the U.S. are classified as medically underserved. If you live in one of them and need to see a rheumatologist, your options used to be: drive three hours, wait four months, or go without. Telehealth collapses that first barrier entirely.

It also helps people who cannot easily leave home: parents of newborns, people with mobility limitations, caregivers for elderly relatives, and anyone without reliable transportation. Missed appointments due to transportation are a well-documented reason for poor chronic disease control. Removing the drive removes the problem.

### 2. Lower Cost, Both Direct and Indirect

A virtual urgent care visit typically runs $40–$75 out of pocket without insurance. An in-person urgent care visit averages closer to $150–$200, and an ER visit for a non-emergency issue can exceed $1,000.

The indirect savings are often bigger. A one-hour in-person appointment with a 45-minute drive each way and a half-day off work is not really a one-hour appointment. For hourly workers, that lost wage can exceed the cost of the visit itself.

### 3. It Works Well for a Specific Set of Conditions

Telehealth is not "almost as good" as in-person care across the board. For certain problems, it is genuinely equivalent or better:

- **Mental health:** Therapy and psychiatry via video have strong evidence behind them for depression, anxiety, and medication management. Many patients actually disclose more when they are in their own space.
- **Chronic disease follow-ups:** Diabetes, hypertension, and asthma check-ins where you are reviewing home readings and adjusting medication.
- **Prescription management:** Refills, dose adjustments, side effect checks.
- **Minor acute issues:** Sinus infections, uncomplicated UTIs, cold sores, seasonal allergies, mild rashes.
- **Follow-up after a procedure:** Wound checks via photo, suture removal planning, pathology review.

### 4. Less Exposure to Illness

If you are immunocompromised, undergoing chemotherapy, or caring for someone who is, avoiding a waiting room full of coughing strangers is not a minor perk. It is a clinical benefit.

### 5. Better Documentation and Follow-Up

Virtual visits are typically recorded in the chart with the same rigor as in-person ones, and messaging portals create a written trail. If you have ever tried to remember what a doctor told you three weeks ago, having it typed out in your patient portal is a real advantage.

## The Genuine Downsides of Telehealth

### 1. The Physical Exam Is Missing — And That Matters

This is the biggest limitation, and no amount of high-definition video fixes it. A doctor cannot palpate your abdomen, listen to your heart, check your lymph nodes, or feel a lump through a screen.

Some conditions are nearly impossible to assess remotely:

- Chest pain that could be cardiac
- Abdominal pain where the location and tenderness matter
- New lumps, bumps, or joint swelling
- Anything requiring a stethoscope, otoscope, or hands
- Neurological exams testing reflexes, strength, or sensation

**Common mistake to avoid:** Downplaying symptoms to fit the format. If you have chest pressure, severe abdominal pain, or sudden weakness, do not book a video visit. Call emergency services.

### 2. Technology and Connection Problems

A pixelated video or a dropped call can turn a 15-minute visit into a frustrating waste of a copay. Audio-only visits are sometimes the only option, and while they are better than nothing, they remove visual cues that clinicians rely on.

Before your visit, test your camera, microphone, and internet connection. Most platforms have a test mode. Use it five minutes early, not two minutes late.

### 3. Regulatory and Licensing Patchwork

Doctors are licensed by state. A physician in Texas generally cannot treat you if you are sitting in California, though temporary pandemic-era flexibilities and interstate compacts have loosened some of this. If you travel frequently or split time between states, confirm your provider can legally see you where you are physically located.

Controlled substance prescribing is another hurdle. Rules around stimulants and certain other medications via telehealth have shifted repeatedly, and many pharmacies will not fill a controlled substance prescription from an out-of-state virtual prescriber.

### 4. It's Easy to Get Low-Quality Care

The explosion of direct-to-consumer telehealth apps means you can see a stranger in 90 seconds. That convenience cuts both ways. These services are optimized for speed, and speed is not always what good medicine requires.

Red flags to watch for:

- A prescriber who never asks about your other medications or allergies
- No offer to coordinate with your primary care doctor
- Pressure to use a specific pharmacy
- A "diagnosis" delivered without any questions about your history

### 5. Privacy Considerations

Your video visit runs over the internet. Reputable platforms use HIPAA-compliant encryption, but not every app does. Avoid conducting visits on public Wi-Fi in a coffee shop, and check whether the platform you are using states its privacy practices clearly.

### 6. It Can Widen Gaps Instead of Closing Them

Telehealth assumes you have a smartphone, a data plan, a private space, and the digital literacy to navigate an app. People who lack those things — often the same people with the worst health outcomes — get left behind. This is a systemic con worth naming.

## A Practical Checklist: Should You Book Telehealth or Go In Person?

Run through this before you schedule.

**Choose telehealth if:**
- [ ] Your issue is a follow-up on a known condition
- [ ] You need a medication refill or dose adjustment
- [ ] You are discussing lab results or a care plan
- [ ] It's a mental health appointment
- [ ] The problem is visible on camera (rash, eye redness, wound)
- [ ] You've been told to "call if symptoms worsen" and want a quick check
- [ ] Travel, mobility, or caregiving makes leaving home very difficult

**Choose in-person if:**
- [ ] You have chest pain, trouble breathing, or signs of stroke
- [ ] You have severe or worsening abdominal pain
- [ ] You found a new lump, lesion, or swelling
- [ ] You need a physical exam, imaging, or lab work done that day
- [ ] You need a vaccine, injection, or procedure
- [ ] You have a fever with confusion or a very unwell child
- [ ] You've had the same symptom for weeks and no one has examined you

**Before any virtual visit, do this:**
1. Write down your top three concerns, in order. Visits are short.
2. Have your medication list and doses in front of you.
3. Take your vitals if you have a home cuff or thermometer.
4. Sit somewhere with good light and a stable connection.
5. Ask at the end: "What would make you want to see me in person?"

That last question is the single most useful thing you can ask a telehealth provider. It puts the limitations on the table and gives you a clear threshold for escalation.

## How to Get the Most Out of a Virtual Visit

Treat it like a real appointment. Show up on time. Be dressed. Have your questions ready. The patients who get the most from telehealth are the ones who prepare, not the ones who treat it like ordering takeout.

Also, keep your primary care doctor in the loop. Telehealth works best as a supplement to a consistent relationship with a clinician who knows your history — not as a replacement for one. If you use an app-based service, ask them to send notes to your PCP. Most will.

## The Bottom Line

Telehealth is excellent for follow-ups, mental health, chronic disease management, and minor acute problems. It is poor for anything requiring hands, ears, or a stethoscope, and dangerous for emergencies. The question is never "is telehealth good or bad" — it is "is telehealth right for this specific problem, today."

Use the checklist. Ask the escalation question. Keep a real doctor in your corner. Do those three things and virtual care becomes a genuine asset instead of a gamble.
