## Introduction

You finish a Tuesday afternoon Zoom call, roll your shoulders back, and feel it: that hot, tight band running from the base of your skull down to the inner corner of your shoulder blade. By 6 p.m. it's a dull ache. By Thursday it's a headache behind your right eye.

If that sounds familiar, you're not imagining it. A 2021 systematic review in the *Journal of Occupational Health* found that roughly 45–60% of office workers report neck pain in any given year, and screen time is one of the strongest correlates. But here's the part most articles skip: neck tension from desk work is rarely a "neck problem." It's a load-management problem, a workstation geometry problem, and a breathing problem—usually all three at once.

This guide is the protocol I'd give a friend who works eight hours a day at a laptop and doesn't want to become a stretching-and-foam-rolling hobbyist. It's specific, it's cheap, and it assumes you have about 10 minutes a day to spare.

---

## First, Understand What's Actually Tight

Neck tension has three common drivers, and they need different fixes:

**1. Sustained low-level contraction.** Your deep neck flexors and upper trapezius hold your head up. At 10–15 pounds, your head is heavy. When your monitor sits too low, your neck extensors contract continuously for hours—not hard, but without rest. Muscle fibers that fire at 5% of maximum for four hours straight develop trigger points and refer pain to the skull and shoulder.

**2. Joint stiffness, not muscle tightness.** The upper cervical spine (C1–C2) loses rotation range when you stare at a screen fixed in one plane. What feels like "tight muscles" is often a stiff joint with normal muscle tone around it. Stretching that muscle does nothing.

**3. Referred pain from the shoulder girdle.** The levator scapulae—the muscle running from your top four cervical vertebrae to the top corner of your shoulder blade—refers pain directly to the angle of your neck and behind the eye. It gets cranky when your shoulders round forward, which happens automatically when your keyboard is too far away.

The practical takeaway: before you stretch anything, check your setup. Most people are trying to stretch their way out of a workstation problem.

## The 20-Minute Workstation Audit

Do this once, today. You need a tape measure and a mirror or a phone camera.

### Checklist

- [ ] **Monitor height:** The top third of your screen should be at eye level. Measure from desk surface to the center of your screen—it should be roughly 15–20 cm above the desk for a typical laptop on a stand. If you're on a bare laptop, you are looking *down* at roughly 15–20 degrees all day. That alone explains most tension.
- [ ] **Monitor distance:** 50–75 cm from your eyes (about an arm's length). Closer than 50 cm and your neck drifts forward; farther and you lean in.
- [ ] **Keyboard position:** Elbows at 90–100 degrees, wrists neutral. If your keyboard is more than 15 cm from the front edge of the desk, you're reaching—which rounds the shoulders and loads the levator scapulae.
- [ ] **Laptop users:** A £15–25 laptop stand plus a £20–40 external keyboard and mouse is the single highest-return purchase in this entire article. A bare laptop cannot be ergonomically correct for sustained work. Full stop.
- [ ] **Chair height:** Feet flat, knees at 90 degrees, hips slightly higher than knees. If your chair has no adjustable height, a firm cushion works.
- [ ] **Armrests:** Set so your shoulders relax *down*, not shrugged up. If armrests push your shoulders toward your ears, lower them or remove them.

### The phone test

Set your phone on a stack of books at eye level, record a 30-second video of yourself working from the side. Look for: ears in front of shoulders (forward head), chin jutting, shoulders rounded. This 30-second video is more diagnostic than any online questionnaire.

## The Myth That Keeps You Stretching the Wrong Thing

**Myth: "My neck is tight, so I need to stretch it."**

Here's the problem. Aggressively stretching the upper trapezius—the classic ear-to-shoulder pull—often *increases* tension in people with forward head posture. Why? Because that muscle is already being passively lengthened by your rounded posture all day. It's not short; it's overloaded and weak. Stretching an already-lengthened, overworked muscle gives temporary relief and zero lasting change.

What actually helps: strengthening the muscles that hold your head in a neutral position (deep neck flexors, lower trapezius, serratus anterior) and mobilizing the joints that are actually stiff (upper cervical rotation, thoracic extension).

A simple test: sit tall, tuck your chin straight back (not down) like you're making a double chin, and hold 10 seconds. If that feels like effort, your deep neck flexors are weak—and that's your target, not your trapezius.

## The Daily 8-Minute Protocol

This is the routine I'd actually prescribe. Do it once mid-afternoon, ideally after 90 minutes of continuous screen work. Total time: 8 minutes.

**1. Chin tucks — 2 sets of 10, 5-second holds (90 seconds).**
Sit tall. Glide your head straight back—imagine a string pulling the back of your skull backward, not upward. You should feel a stretch at the base of the skull, not in the front of the throat. This trains the deep neck flexors.

**2. Wall angels — 2 sets of 8 (2 minutes).**
Stand with your back, head, and heels against a wall. Slide your arms up and down like a snow angel, keeping wrists and elbows touching the wall. Most people can't get their arms overhead without their ribs flaring. Go only as far as you can without arching your lower back. This is brutal the first week and transformative by week four.

**3. Thoracic extension over a chair back — 8 slow reps (90 seconds).**
Sit in a chair with a firm backrest. Interlace your hands behind your head and extend your upper back over the top of the chair back, exhaling as you go. This addresses the stiff mid-back that forces your neck to compensate.

**4. Levator scapulae stretch — 2 × 30 seconds each side (2 minutes).**
Turn your head 45 degrees away from the side you're stretching, look slightly down toward your opposite armpit, and gently pull your head forward with your hand. You should feel it at the top corner of the shoulder blade. Gentle—this is not a competition.

**5. Doorway chest stretch — 2 × 30 seconds (1 minute).**
Forearms on the doorframe at shoulder height, step through gently. Tight pecs pull the shoulders forward, which loads the neck. This is the cheapest fix in the routine.

**6. Breathing reset — 60 seconds.**
Sit tall. Inhale through your nose for 4 seconds, exhale for 6. Do this six times. Shallow chest breathing recruits your scalenes and upper traps as accessory breathing muscles—which is exactly the tension pattern you're fighting. Slow nasal exhales downshift that.

## The Micro-Break Rule That Actually Works

"Take breaks every hour" is advice nobody follows. Here's a version that survives contact with a real workday.

Set a timer for **50 minutes**. When it goes off, do **one** of the following for 60–90 seconds—not all of them, just one:

- 10 slow chin tucks
- Stand up and walk to fill your water bottle (the walk matters more than the water)
- 5 slow shoulder rolls *backward*, with an exhale on each
- Look at something 6 meters away for 20 seconds (this also resets your eyes, which drive a lot of neck tension through visual strain)

The point isn't the exercise. It's interrupting the sustained contraction pattern. Muscle tension accumulates in unbroken blocks of time; 90 seconds of movement every 50 minutes cuts those blocks into manageable pieces. If you do nothing else from this article, do this.

## Sleep and Screen Position: The Overlooked Half

You spend 8 hours at your desk and 7–8 hours in bed. Most people optimize the first and ignore the second.

**Pillow height:** Your pillow should fill the gap between your mattress and your neck so your head is level, not propped up or dropped back. Back sleepers: roughly 8–10 cm of loft. Side sleepers: enough to keep your nose aligned with the center of your chest—often 10–12 cm, and a contoured pillow (the Cervical-type shapes, £25–60) genuinely helps some people. Stomach sleeping forces your neck into rotation for hours; if you can't change it, use a very thin pillow or none.

**Phone posture:** Holding your phone at chest height means your neck flexes roughly 45–60 degrees—the equivalent of hanging a 20 kg weight off your cervical spine in some biomechanical models. Raise the phone to eye level. Your neck will notice within a week.

**The 90-minute rule:** If you wake with more neck stiffness than you went to bed with, your pillow or sleep position is a bigger contributor than your desk. Fix that first.

## What Most Guides Get Wrong

**1. They treat the neck in isolation.** Your neck sits between a stiff mid-back and a rounded shoulder girdle. If you don't address thoracic mobility and scapular strength, neck stretches are a bucket with a hole in it. The wall angel and chair extension do more for most desk workers than any neck stretch.

**2. They recommend stretching over strengthening.** Passive stretching feels good for 20 minutes and changes nothing structurally. The evidence consistently favors loading—chin tucks, rowing variations, wall angels—for lasting change in postural neck pain. Stretch to feel better today; strengthen to feel better in a month.

**3. They ignore breathing.** Up to a third of chronic neck tension cases involve overactive accessory breathing muscles—scalenes and upper traps doing the diaphragm's job. If your shoulders rise when you inhale, no amount of stretching will fix the tension, because you're re-creating it with every breath. The 4-6 breathing drill is not a relaxation add-on; it's a mechanical correction.

**4. They pretend a single fix exists.** There's no magic pillow, no single stretch, no £400 chair that solves this. It's setup + movement frequency + strength + sleep position, in roughly that order of impact. The setup is a one-time 20-minute job. The rest is a habit.

---

## When to See Someone

Most desk-related neck tension responds to the above within 2–4 weeks. See a physiotherapist or doctor if you have: pain radiating down the arm with numbness or tingling, weakness in the hand or arm, unrelenting night pain, or tension accompanied by dizziness or visual changes. Those patterns point to nerve involvement or other causes that self-management won't touch.

For everyone else: fix the monitor height today, set the 50-minute timer, and do the 8-minute routine four times this week. That's the whole intervention. The rest is repetition.
