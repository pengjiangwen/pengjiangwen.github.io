## Introduction

A few years ago, a friend of mine named Dana blamed her constant exhaustion on her job. She drank more coffee, slept more on weekends, and pushed through. Then her hair started shedding in the shower, and her hands tingled at night. Her doctor ran a simple blood test: vitamin B12 deficiency. Three months of supplements and dietary changes later, she felt like herself again—and she was frustrated she'd waited so long.

Dana's story isn't unusual. Vitamin deficiencies develop slowly, and their symptoms are easy to blame on stress, aging, or a busy life. But your body is remarkably good at sending signals before things get serious. The trick is learning to read them.

This post covers the most common signs of vitamin deficiency, which nutrients are usually involved, and what to do next. One important note upfront: these symptoms have many possible causes, so use this as a guide for conversations with your doctor—not a diagnosis.

## Why Vitamin Deficiencies Are More Common Than You Think

Modern diets can be plentiful in calories yet poor in nutrients. Highly processed foods lose much of their vitamin content during manufacturing. Restricted diets—vegan, keto, gluten-free—can inadvertently cut out key nutrients. And certain life stages and conditions (pregnancy, aging, digestive disorders, some medications) raise your requirements or reduce absorption.

According to research published in *The Lancet* and other major journals, deficiencies in vitamin D, B12, and iron (a mineral, but closely tied to vitamin C and B-vitamin status) are among the most widespread worldwide. Even in wealthy countries, low-level insufficiency—not full deficiency—is surprisingly common.

## The 7 Most Telling Signs of Vitamin Deficiency

### 1. Persistent Fatigue That Sleep Doesn't Fix

Everyone gets tired. The red flag is fatigue that lingers for weeks despite adequate sleep, or that gets worse with exertion.

**Likely culprits:** Vitamin B12, vitamin D, iron, and vitamin B9 (folate).

B12 and folate are essential for red blood cell production. Without enough, you develop anemia, which means your tissues get less oxygen. Vitamin D deficiency has also been linked to chronic fatigue in multiple studies.

**What to do:** Ask your doctor for a CBC (complete blood count), ferritin, B12, and vitamin D panel. Don't self-prescribe high-dose B12 injections before testing—you want an accurate baseline.

### 2. Tingling or Numbness in Hands and Feet

That "pins and needles" feeling that doesn't go away after you change position deserves attention.

**Likely culprit:** Vitamin B12.

B12 is critical for the myelin sheath—the protective coating around your nerves. When it's depleted, nerve signaling falters, causing tingling, numbness, or burning in the extremities. Left untreated, nerve damage can become permanent.

This symptom is especially common in vegans and vegetarians (B12 occurs naturally almost exclusively in animal foods), people over 60 (absorption declines with age), and anyone taking long-term acid-reducing medications or metformin.

### 3. Muscle Cramps, Bone Pain, and Frequent Fractures

If your muscles ache or spasm, or if you've broken a bone from a minor fall, your skeleton may be trying to tell you something.

**Likely culprits:** Vitamin D, calcium, and magnesium.

Vitamin D governs calcium absorption. Without it, your body pulls calcium from your bones, weakening them over time. Muscle cramps and bone tenderness are classic early signals. Low vitamin D is also associated with depression, immune problems, and slow wound healing.

**Actionable tip:** Ten to twenty minutes of midday sun on bare skin several times a week helps most people synthesize vitamin D. In winter or northern latitudes, food sources (fatty fish, fortified dairy, egg yolks) and supplements often become necessary.

### 4. Hair Loss and Brittle Nails

Shedding 50–100 hairs a day is normal. Finding clumps on your pillow or in the shower drain is not.

**Likely culprits:** Vitamin B7 (biotin), zinc, iron, and vitamin D.

Hair and nails are among the fastest-growing tissues in your body, which makes them sensitive to nutrient shortages. They're often the first to suffer and the last to recover. If your nails have developed ridges, white spots, or a spoon-like shape, that's worth mentioning to your doctor.

### 5. Slow Wound Healing and Easy Bruising

A paper cut that lingers for weeks or bruises that appear without explanation can point to a nutrient gap.

**Likely culprits:** Vitamin C and vitamin K.

Vitamin C is required for collagen synthesis, the scaffolding of new skin tissue. Vitamin K is essential for blood clotting. Both deficiencies are rarer in developed countries, but low-level insufficiency still occurs—especially in smokers, people with poor diets, and those with alcohol use disorders.

### 6. Vision Problems, Especially at Night

Struggling to see in dim light, or noticing dry, gritty eyes, is a classic sign that shows up long before any serious eye damage.

**Likely culprit:** Vitamin A.

Vitamin A produces rhodopsin, a pigment in the retina that enables night vision. It also keeps the cornea moist and healthy. Vitamin A deficiency remains a leading cause of preventable blindness globally, though in developed countries it's more often a mild insufficiency.

**Food fix:** Sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach, and liver are rich sources. Pair plant sources with a little fat—vitamin A is fat-soluble—to boost absorption.

### 7. Mood Changes, Brain Fog, and Memory Lapses

Feeling unusually irritable, forgetful, or "foggy" isn't always stress. It can be nutritional.

**Likely culprits:** B vitamins (especially B12, B6, and folate), vitamin D.

B vitamins help synthesize neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine. Vitamin D receptors exist throughout the brain. Studies have repeatedly linked low levels of both to depression, cognitive decline, and mood instability—particularly in older adults.

## Who's Most at Risk?

You don't need to worry equally about all deficiencies. Certain groups face higher odds:

- **Vegans and strict vegetarians:** B12, iron, zinc, omega-3s
- **Adults over 60:** B12, vitamin D, calcium
- **Pregnant and breastfeeding women:** folate, iron, vitamin D, B12
- **People with celiac disease, Crohn's, or IBS:** fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and B12 due to malabsorption
- **Anyone taking metformin, PPIs, or certain diuretics:** B12, magnesium, potassium
- **People with darker skin living in northern climates:** vitamin D
- **Heavy alcohol users:** thiamine (B1), folate, B12, vitamin C

If you fall into one or more of these categories, routine testing is a smart move—even if you feel fine.

## What to Do If You Recognize These Signs

1. **Don't guess—test.** Blood work is the only way to confirm a deficiency and identify which nutrient is low. Symptoms overlap heavily, and taking the wrong supplement wastes money and delays real answers.
2. **Change your plate first.** Whole foods deliver vitamins in combinations and forms your body handles well. A varied diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats covers most bases.
3. **Supplement strategically, not randomly.** High-dose supplements can be harmful—too much vitamin A is toxic to the liver, and excess B6 can cause nerve damage. Take only what your doctor recommends.
4. **Recheck levels.** After 8–12 weeks of treatment, follow-up testing confirms you're back on track.
5. **Address the root cause.** If absorption is the problem (not intake), supplements alone may not solve it. Digestive conditions, medications, and surgeries all need to be part of the conversation.

## The Bottom Line

Your body rarely fails silently. Fatigue, tingling nerves, thinning hair, and low mood are not just annoyances to power through—they're data. Vitamin deficiencies are common, treatable, and often reversible when caught early. The mistake is waiting until symptoms become severe.

If two or more of the signs above sound familiar, book that appointment. A simple blood test can turn months of unexplained misery into a straightforward fix. Dana wishes she'd done it a year sooner—and you don't have to repeat her delay.
